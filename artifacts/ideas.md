## 1. Measuring overseer discretion: porting the alignment-discretion metrics onto EU AI Act human oversight  _(transplant, score=0.7)_
TRANSPLANT. Method source: 'AI Alignment at Your Discretion' (doi:10.1145/3715275.3732194) formalizes 'discretion' borrowed from legal theory and, crucially, gives a measurable instrument: principle-specific preference functions evaluated by an oracle, and the metrics Discretion Arbitrariness (how often an annotator contradicts the consensus of stated principles), Principle Supremacy / Principle Priority weights (an ELO-style ranking revealing which principles actually dominate decisions), and Discretion Discrepancy (Kendall-tau distance between human and algorithmic discretion). It empirically shows human annotators contradict principle consensus ~15-29% of the time and that operational guidelines ('be helpful') outrank stated fundamental rights. Problem/domain source: 'Institutionalised distrust and human oversight' (doi:10.1007/s00146-023-01777-z), which argues EU AI Act Article 14 mandates effective human oversight but does not define it, that human overseers are unreliable (automation bias vs. algorithm aversion), and introduces first-degree vs. second-degree overseer roles. The transplant takes the discretion-measurement apparatus built for RLHF annotators and applies it to human overseers of deployed high-risk AI systems: treat the oversight policy's stated principles (safety, non-discrimination, fundamental rights, efficiency) as the 'constitution,' use an oracle to compute principle-specific preferred actions for logged override/approval decisions, and compute Arbitrariness (how often overseers override against their own stated principle hierarchy), Principle Priority (do overseers in practice privilege throughput over rights?), and Discrepancy between first-degree and second-degree overseers. This yields a concrete, auditable metric for whether Article 14 oversight is 'effective' or merely rubber-stamping, and lets regulators detect automation-bias signatures quantitatively. Could be paired with the redress-mechanisms framing (doi:10.1017/cfl.2025.9) to connect measured overseer arbitrariness to ex-post accountability. Novel because alignment-discretion metrics have only been applied to model-training annotation, never to the governance question of legally-mandated human oversight.

- **Builds on:** [[doi:10.1145/3715275.3732194]] [[doi:10.1007/s00146-023-01777-z]] [[doi:10.1017/cfl.2025.9]]
- **Novelty:** novel — No prior work applies the specific alignment-discretion measurement apparatus (Discretion Arbitrariness, Principle Supremacy/Priority ELO ranking, Discretion Discrepancy via Kendall-tau) to legally-mandated human overseers of deployed high-risk AI under EU AI Act Article 14. Both source works are confirmed real and currently siloed: the method source (Buyl, Khalaf et al., "AI Alignment at Your Discretion," FAccT '25, arxiv 2502.10441) applies these metrics ONLY to RLHF/alignment-dataset annotators; the domain source (Laux, "Institutionalised distrust...," 10.1007/s00146-023-01777-z) supplies the first-/second-degree overseer taxonomy and the automation-bias-vs-algorithm-aversion framing but proposes no quantitative instrument. The strongest novelty signal: the dedicated Article 14 compliance-testing paper ("On the Complexities of Testing for Compliance with Human Oversight Requirements," arxiv 2504.03300) explicitly states it does NOT propose quantitative metrics for overseer discretion, arbitrariness, principle prioritization, or automation-bias signatures, and frames operationalizing these as an open challenge needing future HCI/psychology work — exactly the gap this transplant fills. Adjacent but distinct work exists: industry/governance guides list override-rate and override-reason metrics; academic work on oversight-cost/reliability auditing (arxiv 2603.24582 "Stochastic Gap"), the flaws of human-oversight policies (arxiv 2109.05067), and empirical override-following studies (HART/Durham; Centre Court arxiv 2401.16754). None port the principle-consensus/arbitrariness/ELO-priority/Kendall-tau-discrepancy construct to overseer logs, and I found no paper bridging the two source works. Caveat: the fast-moving 2026 literature means a very recent preprint could exist that I did not surface.
- **Feasibility:** Moderately feasible as a methods-contribution paper; substantially harder as a real-world regulatory audit. The conceptual port is clean and the metrics are well-defined and reusable. Three binding constraints. (1) Data access: the metrics require logged override/approval decisions with enough context to recompute a principle-preferred action; such logs live inside deploying organizations or regulators and are rarely public, so a first paper would likely need a synthetic/simulated oversight task or a lab study with mock overseers (tractable proof-of-concept) rather than field logs. (2) Oracle validity is the central risk: the source paper used an LLM oracle over text-pair preferences, but high-risk override decisions (credit, medical triage, hiring, content) are domain-specific, multi-attribute, and often lack a defensible ground-truth principle-preferred action. The oracle effectively encodes a contested normative ranking of safety vs non-discrimination vs efficiency, so 'Discretion Discrepancy' measures distance from the oracle's contestable values, not from truth — results need heavy sensitivity analysis across oracle specifications. (3) Constitution construction: real oversight policies rarely state a machine-readable principle hierarchy, so the 'constitution' must be elicited/reconstructed, adding researcher discretion. Mitigations make a credible FAccT/AIES-style paper achievable in months; a deployable Article 14 audit tool is a multi-year, access-gated effort.
- **Assumptions:** Overseer override/approval decisions are logged with sufficient decision context; the oversight policy's principles (safety, non-discrimination, fundamental rights, efficiency) can be turned into principle-specific preference functions; an oracle can compute reasonably valid principle-preferred actions for domain-specific high-risk decisions; first-degree and second-degree overseer decisions are separately attributable; metric outputs are interpreted relative to the oracle's contestable normative ranking, not as ground truth.

## 2. Regulator sandbagging: transplanting password-locking and strategic-underperformance evals into the regulatory-markets red-teaming pipeline  _(transplant, score=0.62)_
TRANSPLANT. Method source: 'AI Sandbagging' (doi:10.48550/arxiv.2406.07358) defines strategic underperformance on evaluations, distinguishes developer-sandbagging from system-sandbagging, and gives an empirical toolkit: prompt-induced selective failure on dangerous-capability sets (WMDP) while holding control benchmarks (MMLU) constant, password-locking via LoRA fine-tuning that hides capabilities until a trigger, capability-profile emulation of a weaker model, and a detection method that plots accuracy vs. question difficulty to expose suspiciously flat profiles. Problem/domain source: 'Regulatory Markets' (doi:10.48550/arxiv.2304.04914) proposes government-licensed private regulators who sell red-teaming/auditing services to AI firms, and explicitly flags the core failure mode that these private regulators may collude with the firms they audit (independence/capture risk). The transplant moves sandbagging up one level: from a model strategically underperforming, to a captured private regulator (or a firm's internal eval) strategically under-reporting capabilities to its government licensor. Build a formal/empirical model of 'audit sandbagging' in the regulatory-markets setting: define the analogue of password-locking (a captured regulator runs the 'real' eval suite but reports a capability-profile-emulated weaker model to the government), adapt the difficulty-vs-accuracy detection signature into an audit-integrity test the licensor can run with retained hold-out probes, and quantify how much white-box / structured access the government needs to detect it. Combine with the antitrust-collaboration framing (doi:10.70777/si.v2i6.16439) to ask whether mandated cross-regulator red-teaming and information-sharing (the paper's safe-harbor proposals) make audit-sandbagging detectable. Novel because sandbagging research has stayed at the model level; transplanting it to institutional auditors in a regulatory-markets design directly operationalizes that paper's unaddressed capture risk.

- **Builds on:** [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2406.07358]] [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2304.04914]] [[doi:10.70777/si.v2i6.16439]] [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2601.11916]]
- **Novelty:** novel — The specific transplant — lifting the sandbagging toolkit (password-locking, capability-profile emulation, difficulty-vs-accuracy detection signature) from the model level to the institutional level of a CAPTURED PRIVATE REGULATOR under-reporting to its government licensor in a regulatory-markets design — does not appear to exist in the literature. No paper uses an "audit/regulator/institutional sandbagging" framing. However, strong adjacent work exists that bounds the novelty:

1) Both source papers are real and say what the idea claims. "AI Sandbagging" (2406.07358) gives the empirical toolkit (selective WMDP failure with MMLU held constant, LoRA password-locking, weaker-model emulation, difficulty-vs-accuracy detection). "Regulatory Markets" (2304.04914, Hadfield/Clark) proposes licensed private regulators and explicitly flags collusion/capture as the core failure mode — so the proposal correctly identifies a gap the source paper leaves open.

2) Closest prior work — model level, NOT institutional: "Auditing Games for Sandbagging" (AISI, arXiv 2512.07810) and "Noise Injection Reveals Hidden Capabilities" (2412.01784) build red-team/blue-team detection of sandbagging IN MODELS. They do not model a captured auditor strategically under-reporting to a licensor. Notably AISI found the blue team could NOT reliably discriminate sandbaggers from benign models — a result that directly threatens the proposal's assumption that a difficulty-vs-accuracy signature transplants cleanly to the audit layer.

3) Closest prior work — institutional/economic level, but no eval mechanics: "Both eyes open: Vigilant Incentives help Regulatory Markets improve AI Safety" (2303.03174) already game-theoretically models when private regulators in a regulatory market collude vs. genuinely regulate, and how government oversight incentives ("vigilant incentives") deter capture. This is the same institutional collusion target the proposal wants to formalize, but it stops at high-level effort/payoff dynamics and does NOT operationalize capability-evaluation detection (hold-out probes, accuracy-vs-difficulty audit-integrity test). The proposal's distinctive contribution is precisely bolting the sandbagging detection mechanics onto this collusion layer.

4) Standard audit-economics literature on auditor independence/collusion/lowballing and "Outsider Oversight" (2206.04737) cover captured-auditor dynamics generically but not the AI-capability-eval-specific detection signatures.

5) The 4th grounded doc (2601.11916, "Expanding External Access To Frontier AI Models For Dangerous Capability Evaluations") is highly relevant to the proposal's "how much white-box/structured access does the licensor need" sub-question and would supply a real access-tiers framework rather than requiring it to be invented.

Net: the conceptual transplant (sandbagging-as-institutional-fraud + a concrete licensor-side detection test) is novel, but it sits in a crowded neighborhood (2303.03174 owns the collusion-game framing; 2512.07810 owns the detection-game framing). It is novel-but-narrow, not wide open.
- **Feasibility:** Mixed; the framing/formal contribution is feasible, the empirical detection claim is the weak point.

FEASIBLE parts: (a) A formal model of audit sandbagging — a captured regulator/internal eval that runs the true suite but reports a capability-profile-emulated weaker model — is straightforwardly buildable as a principal-agent / signalling game on top of the existing "Vigilant Incentives" (2303.03174) regulatory-markets game. (b) A licensor-side audit-integrity protocol using retained hold-out probes is conceptually clean and can be simulated end-to-end: take WMDP/MMLU, have a "captured auditor" report emulated-weaker-model scores, and test whether the licensor's hold-out probes plus an accuracy-vs-difficulty test flag it. The original sandbagging paper's artifacts (password-locked models, WMDP/MMLU, the difficulty-binning detector) are public, so an MVP is a small fine-tuning + benchmark-replotting exercise, not a frontier-scale effort. (c) Mapping access requirements onto the tiers in 2601.11916 is a policy-synthesis task.

WEAK / HIGH-RISK assumptions: (1) The central detection signature may not survive the transplant. The difficulty-vs-accuracy "flat profile" tell was demonstrated against naive prompt-induced sandbagging; AISI's auditing-games result (2512.07810) shows detection is unreliable even at the model level, and a captured INSTITUTION is a far more capable adversary that can calibrate reported scores to mimic a plausible difficulty curve, defeating the signature. The proposal somewhat hand-waves this. (2) Hold-out probes only work if the licensor's probes are drawn from a distribution the auditor cannot reconstruct or front-run; a captured auditor with full model access can often elicit and memorize the elicitation behavior, collapsing the hold-out advantage — this needs to be modeled as the core adversarial game, not assumed away. (3) The model-level vs institution-level disanalogy is real: a regulator's "report" is an aggregate document, not per-question model outputs, so the per-item difficulty detector may have no observable to bind to unless the licensor mandates item-level logging — making the contribution as much about disclosure/logging mandates as about the statistical test. (4) Whether mandated cross-regulator red-teaming/info-sharing (the antitrust safe-harbor proposals) actually makes sandbagging detectable is an empirical/institutional-design claim that the proposed simulation can illustrate but not establish. Overall: a credible formal-model-plus-simulation paper is achievable by a small team in months; a robust empirical detection guarantee is unlikely and should not be promised.
- **Assumptions:** 1) Assumes the difficulty-vs-accuracy detection signature and hold-out probes are transferable from a single model to an institutional auditor's aggregate report — questionable given (a) the auditor is a stronger, adaptive adversary, (b) AISI already found model-level detection unreliable, and (c) institutional reports may not expose per-item outputs the detector needs. 2) Assumes the licensor can retain probes the captured auditor cannot reconstruct despite the auditor having deeper model access than the licensor. 3) Assumes "developer-sandbagging vs system-sandbagging" cleanly maps to "captured-regulator vs honest-regulator-fooled-by-firm"; the institutional layer adds a third party (firm) so it is really a three-player game, not a relabeled two-player one. 4) Assumes regulatory markets are a live enough governance proposal that operationalizing their capture risk has policy impact — plausible but the framework remains largely theoretical/un-adopted. 5) Treats the antitrust/safe-harbor cross-regulator info-sharing remedy as compatible with audit-integrity testing; interaction effects (info-sharing could also help auditors coordinate their collusion) are not addressed. 6) Relevance to AI-safety∩AI-policy is high and genuine: it targets a named, unaddressed failure mode in a real governance proposal and ties it to a concrete, mechanistic safety eval.
- **⚠ Dual-use note:** Mild, flag-only — do not withhold. By formalizing how a captured auditor could emulate a weaker capability profile and calibrate reported scores to mimic a plausible difficulty curve, the work doubles as a recipe for evading the very detector it proposes (an adversary could read the "how the licensor detects you" section as "how to pass the licensor's hold-out test"). This is the standard red-team/blue-team duality already present in the source sandbagging and auditing-games papers, and is low-severity: it concerns gaming institutional audits, not eliciting dangerous model capabilities directly, and the underlying detection-evasion ideas are already public. Mitigations: emphasize defender-side protocol design (item-level logging mandates, unpredictable probe sampling, cross-regulator verification) over a transferable evasion playbook, and avoid releasing a turnkey "captured-auditor" emulation pipeline. No hard info-hazard; publish with the usual responsible-disclosure framing.

## 3. From Discretion to Guardrail: Encoding Alignment-Discretion Metrics as Policy-as-Code Conformity Controls in a Layered Governance Stack  _(combination, score=0.62)_
Problem and gap: 'AI Alignment at Your Discretion' (3732194, cluster 5) formalizes and measures the latitude annotators exercise when alignment principles conflict, introducing metrics — Discretion Arbitrariness (DA), Principle Supremacy/Priority weights, and Discretion Discrepancy (DD via Kendall tau) — and shows empirically that human annotators disagree with principle consensus 28.9% of the time on HH-RLHF, that off-the-shelf models poorly mirror human discretion, and that operational principles like 'be helpful' systematically outrank fundamental-rights principles like freedom of expression. These are powerful diagnostics but live entirely inside the research/measurement layer with no path into enforceable governance. Two governance-cluster papers supply the missing enforcement and conformity scaffolding. 'Policy-as-Code for AI Risk Governance' (jocaaa.2026.35.03.19, cluster 4) shows how to compile governance controls into machine-readable, version-controlled platform primitives embedded in CI/CD gates and model-registry policy manifests, with four operational metrics (exception rate, time-to-approval, policy drift, incident response time) and a human-review escape hatch for ambiguous cases. 'A five-layer framework for AI governance' (tg-03-2025-0065, cluster 1) provides the missing-middle structure that maps high-level law/standards down through standardized assessment procedures (Layer 3), assessment tools/metrics (Layer 4), to certification (Layer 5). Combination: turn the 3732194 discretion metrics into concrete Layer-4 assessment tools that are then enforced as Policy-as-Code guardrails. Method: (1) Define a standardized assessment procedure (Layer 3 of tg-03-2025-0065) for 'discretion conformity' that computes DA, Principle Priority weights, and DD on a developer's preference/RLHF data against a declared principle hierarchy; (2) encode pass/fail thresholds for those metrics as policy-as-code manifests (jocaaa.2026) that gate model promotion in the registry — e.g., block a model whose Principle Priority weights rank fundamental-rights principles below operational ones, or whose annotator DA exceeds a bound — with the documented human-review path for genuinely contested value conflicts (which 3732194 shows are ~15-20% conflict cases); (3) feed the resulting machine-generated audit evidence into the Layer-5 certification ecosystem, giving alignment discretion a verifiable conformity trail. Expected contribution: closes the loop between a pure measurement paper and deployable governance — the first proposal to make annotator/model value-prioritization a *certifiable, continuously enforced* property rather than an opaque internal choice, directly answering the 3732194 worry that discretion is 'largely unexamined' and the jocaaa.2026 worry that probabilistic value judgments resist executable rules. Evaluation: reproduce the 3732194 metrics on HH-RLHF and PKU-SafeRLHF, wire them into a prototype policy-as-code registry gate, and measure the jocaaa.2026 operational metrics (exception rate, drift) to show which discretion violations are caught automatically vs. routed to human review, validated against the layered-framework case-study method of tg-03-2025-0065.

- **Builds on:** [[doi:10.1145/3715275.3732194]] [[doi:10.48047/jocaaa.2026.35.03.19]] [[doi:10.1108/tg-03-2025-0065]]
- **Novelty:** novel — All three grounding papers are real and accurately characterized. "AI Alignment at Your Discretion" (Buyl et al., FAccT 2025 / arXiv 2502.10441) does introduce Discretion Arbitrariness, Principle Supremacy/Priority, and Discretion Discrepancy (Kendall-tau) metrics on HH-RLHF and PKU-SafeRLHF, with the ~28.9% disagreement and operational-over-fundamental-rights findings as described. The Policy-as-Code (jocaaa.2026) and five-layer governance (tg-03-2025-0065) papers are also real and supply the enforcement/conformity scaffolding claimed.

The specific combination — turning discretion metrics into Layer-4 assessment tools enforced as policy-as-code registry promotion gates feeding a Layer-5 certification trail — was NOT found anywhere. The nearest neighbors are clearly distinct: (a) Value Imprint (NeurIPS 2024, arXiv 2411.11937) and SEAL (arXiv 2408.10270) audit human values / value-alignment errors in RLHF datasets but stay entirely in the measurement/audit layer — they do not enforce, gate, or certify, which is exactly the gap this idea targets; (b) Policy-as-Prompt / "AI Agent Code of Conduct" (arXiv 2509.23994) compiles governance rules into runtime prompt guardrails for agents, explicitly NOT covering discretion metrics, preference-data audits, or registry gates (confirmed via fetch); (c) EU AI Act conformity literature (arXiv 2602.00078, Bench-2-CoP arXiv 2508.05464, Article 43) discusses the policy-to-machine-enforceable gap and reproducible technical checks but in generic fairness/safety terms, never operationalizing value-prioritization/discretion as a certifiable property. The "first proposal to make annotator/model value-prioritization a certifiable, continuously enforced property" claim holds up against the current literature. Novelty is integrative rather than a new primitive: each component exists, but the wiring is unpublished.
- **Feasibility:** Moderately feasible as a research prototype, with real conceptual hurdles. Strengths: (1) The 3732194 metrics are computational and reproducible — the authors release/describe enough to recompute DA, Principle Priority weights, and DD on HH-RLHF and PKU-SafeRLHF; (2) policy-as-code registry gates (OPA/Rego-style manifests, MLflow/registry promotion hooks, CI/CD checks) are mature, off-the-shelf engineering; (3) the five-layer framework provides a clean mapping skeleton and case-study evaluation method. A graduate team could build the end-to-end prototype in a few months.

Key risks/assumptions: (a) THRESHOLD JUSTIFICATION is the hard problem — the idea assumes one can set defensible pass/fail bounds on DA or a binary "fundamental-rights must outrank operational principles" rule, but the source paper's own finding is that ~15-20% of conflicts are genuinely contested value tradeoffs with no ground-truth ordering; encoding a normative hierarchy into a gate risks substituting the developer's (or regulator's) contested value choice for an "objective" check, which can be gamed or be itself illegitimate. (b) The metrics require a DECLARED PRINCIPLE HIERARCHY as input; most real preference datasets lack per-item principle annotations, so the assessment procedure presupposes a labeling/decomposition step (akin to Constitutional-AI principle tagging) that is itself expensive and discretion-laden — circularity risk. (c) Statistical validity of gating on dataset-level aggregate metrics (a model "promotion" decision based on annotator-pool DA) needs care: the metric describes the data/annotators, not necessarily the trained model's behavior, so blocking model promotion on annotator DA is a questionable causal link unless re-derived on model outputs. (d) The human-review escape hatch needs an operational definition of "genuinely contested," which is exactly the unsolved normative question. Overall feasibility is good for a demonstrative prototype and weaker for a deployable, defensible governance control.
- **Assumptions:** Assumes: the 3732194 metrics are reproducible from public artifacts on HH-RLHF/PKU-SafeRLHF; that preference data can be (or already is) annotated with the principle(s) in conflict so DA/Priority/DD are computable; that meaningful, defensible numeric thresholds exist for at least some discretion violations (e.g., rank-order constraints on fundamental vs. operational principles) even though the source paper shows ~15-20% of conflicts are genuinely contested; that policy-as-code registry/CI-CD gating from jocaaa.2026 and the five-layer mapping from tg-03-2025-0065 can be instantiated in a prototype registry; and that gating model promotion on annotator-data-derived metrics is an acceptable proxy for model-level value behavior (the weakest assumption). Evaluation against the layered-framework case-study method is qualitative.

## 4. Anti-Sandbagging Governance: Making Evaluation-Gaming Legally and Economically Costly Through Mandatory Elicitation Escrow  _(original, score=0.62)_
OPEN PROBLEM TARGETED: The entire frontier-AI regulatory regime conditions interventions (e.g., compute-threshold triggers, deployment gates) on the outcome of dangerous-capability evaluations, yet the trigger itself is gameable. Hubinger-style 'AI Sandbagging' (doi:10.48550/arxiv.2406.07358) demonstrates that frontier models can be prompted or password-locked to selectively underperform on WMDP while passing controls, and explicitly concludes that 'trustworthy evaluations require more than black-box access.' 'Model evaluation for extreme risks' (doi:10.48550/arxiv.2305.15324) lists capability overhang and deceptive alignment among five reasons evaluations miss risks; the external-access taxonomy paper (doi:10.48550/arxiv.2601.11916) confirms black-box methods are unreliable for backdoors but stops at recommending access levels; and the detection-rate model (doi:10.48550/arxiv.2412.15433) admits it does not model adversarial concealment or false negatives from strategic underperformance. NEW DIRECTION: No paper proposes a *governance* mechanism that changes the developer's incentive to sandbag; existing work treats sandbagging as a purely technical detection problem. We propose 'elicitation escrow': a regulatory protocol in which (a) the developer must submit, before evaluation, a cryptographically committed 'capability elicitation contract' (fine-tuning data, scaffolding, password-unlock procedures) to a third-party evaluator who can re-elicit true performance; (b) any post-hoc demonstration that an escrowed model outperformed its self-reported evaluation by more than a tolerance band triggers strict liability and forfeiture of a safety bond, converting sandbagging from a dominant strategy into a costly one. We formalize the escrow as an extension of the survival/hazard model in doi:10.48550/arxiv.2412.15433, adding a strategic-concealment parameter and deriving the bond size that makes truthful elicitation incentive-compatible. CONTRIBUTION: a concrete, implementable bridge between the technical sandbagging literature and frontier-AI law that none of the grounding papers attempt, plus a falsifiable game-theoretic model of when escrow deters concealment. DELIVERABLES: (1) the formal incentive model; (2) a reference legal text mapping escrow obligations onto the access levels of doi:10.48550/arxiv.2601.11916; (3) a small empirical replication showing escrowed re-elicitation recovers locked capabilities at bounded evaluator cost.

- **Builds on:** [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2406.07358]] [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2412.15433]] [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2601.11916]] [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2305.15324]]
- **Novelty:** novel — The specific unified mechanism — pre-evaluation cryptographic ESCROW of an "elicitation contract" (fine-tuning data, scaffolding, password-unlock) + a FORFEITABLE safety bond triggered by a tolerance-band gap between escrowed re-elicitation and self-reported performance + a hazard-model extension deriving the incentive-compatible bond size — was NOT found as a combined proposal. However, every component exists separately, so the contribution is synthesis, not a wholly new primitive.

KEY ADJACENT PRIOR ART:
1. Closest competitor: "AI Sandbagging: Allocating the Risk of Loss for Scheming by AI Systems" (Harvard JOLT). I read it directly. It proposes M&A-style representations & warranties + indemnity/holdbacks, with a REASONABLENESS ("reasonable safeguards") standard — explicitly NOT strict liability, NOT a forfeitable regulatory bond, NOT pre-evaluation elicitation escrow, NO game-theoretic bond-sizing. The proposal's strict-liability + escrow design is a genuine departure but overlaps on the "make sandbagging a contractual/financial risk" intuition, so the idea is not first-to-the-incentive-framing.
2. Sandbagging treated purely as DETECTION: 2406.07358 (password-locking/WMDP), 2412.01784 (noise injection reveals hidden capability), 2512.07810 + AISI "Auditing Games for Sandbagging," 2509.26239 (survival-bandit). None propose a governance/deterrence incentive mechanism.
3. Grounding paper 2601.11916 (external access taxonomy): I fetched it — confirmed it recommends only technical access levels (black/grey/white-box, fine-tuning) and proposes NO escrow, bond, liability, or cryptographic-commitment-of-elicitation mechanism. The proposal's "map escrow obligations onto access levels" is a real gap-fill.
4. Bonds/insurance for AI: catastrophe bonds, nuclear-precedent insurance (2409.06673), government-as-insurer-of-last-resort (2409.06672) — all target HARM compensation/judgment-proofness, not EVALUATION-HONESTY deterrence. Different objective function.
5. Cryptographic commitment: ZK end-to-end pipelines (2503.22573), trustless audits (2404.04500), attestable/TEE audits (2506.23706) commit weights/training-data, but committing the ELICITATION PROCEDURE pre-eval was not found.
6. Pre-registration/declared assumptions: "Declare and Justify" (2411.12820) forces explicit eval assumptions but has no financial bond or escrow re-elicitation.

Conclusion: the incentive-reframing of sandbagging is partially anticipated (Harvard JOLT), but the concrete escrow+bond+formal-bond-sizing protocol mapped onto access levels is novel and unattempted by any single source.
- **Feasibility:** MIXED — strong on the formal/legal deliverables, weak on the technical-cryptographic and political-economy assumptions.

TRACTABLE: (1) The game-theoretic deliverable is very doable — extending a hazard/detection model with a strategic-concealment parameter and deriving a bond B that makes truthful elicitation incentive-compatible is a standard mechanism-design exercise (cf. environmental performance bonds, fidelity/honesty bonds). (2) The empirical replication is the most credible deliverable: password-locked models (2406.07358) and noise-injection recovery (2412.01784) already show locked capabilities can be re-elicited at bounded cost, so a small "escrowed re-elicitation recovers locked capability" demo is low-risk and largely a re-packaging of existing results. (3) A reference legal text mapping escrow obligations onto 2601.11916 access levels is a writing/synthesis task.

KEY ASSUMPTIONS / RISKS:
- VERIFIABILITY GAP (central weakness): the escrow only deters sandbagging the developer KNOWS about and can commit. It cannot escrow an elicitation procedure for a capability the developer has not yet discovered, nor for emergent/post-deployment capability or genuine deceptive-alignment by the MODEL (vs. the developer). The mechanism deters developer-side strategic underperformance but does little for the model-side deceptive-alignment case that 2305.15324 lists — yet the framing implies broad coverage. This is an over-claim that needs scoping.
- "POST-HOC DEMONSTRATION" TRIGGER is the load-bearing and least-developed assumption: strict liability + forfeiture requires a legally robust, low-false-positive way to prove a model "outperformed by more than a tolerance band." Detection literature reports non-trivial false-negative/false-positive rates; a tolerance band that is legally defensible AND deterrence-effective may not exist. Adversarial concealment can target staying within the band.
- CRYPTO COMMITMENT of a "capability elicitation contract" is under-specified: committing a hash of fine-tuning data/scaffolding is easy, but proving the escrowed artifact is the BEST elicitation (not a deliberately weak one the developer submitted) is the same elicitation-completeness problem one level up — the developer can sandbag the escrowed contract itself.
- ENFORCEMENT/JURISDICTION: strict liability + bond forfeiture presupposes a regulator with statutory authority to mandate escrow and seize bonds; current regimes (EU AI Act, SB 53, RAISE Act) do not provide this, so the "reference legal text" is aspirational de lege ferenda, not implementable today.
- Bond-sizing depends on unobservable parameters (developer's private valuation of passing, detection probability) — the derived B is illustrative, not calibratable, so "incentive-compatible" is a model artifact more than a policy guarantee.

Overall: publishable as a position/formal paper with a toy empirical demo; not a near-term deployable regime.
