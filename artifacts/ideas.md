## 1. Capability-Decorrelated Key Risk Indicators: Auditing Frontier Risk-Management Thresholds Against Safetywashing  _(combination, score=0.62)_
Problem and gap: Frontier AI risk-management frameworks operationalize a quantitative risk tolerance into paired Key Risk Indicator (KRI) and Key Control Indicator (KCI) thresholds, using benchmark/eval scores as the measured KRIs that trigger mitigations (Frontier AI Risk Management Framework, doi:10.48550/arxiv.2502.06656). But 'Safetywashing' (doi:10.48550/arxiv.2407.21792) shows empirically that many AI safety benchmarks are highly correlated with general capabilities and training compute (e.g., MT-Bench 78.7% capabilities, TruthfulQA MC1 81.2%, scalable-oversight GPQA 77.7%), so a benchmark used as a KRI can register 'improvement' purely from scaling rather than genuine risk reduction. This creates a hidden failure mode: a KRI threshold can be satisfied by capability gains, signaling 'risk below tolerance' while the underlying hazard (e.g., weaponization, which Safetywashing finds is negatively correlated with capabilities) is unchanged or worse.

Combination / method: (1) Take the KRI/KCI threshold machinery and three-way tolerance relationship from 2502.06656 as the governance scaffold. (2) Apply Safetywashing's PCA + Spearman-correlation-against-capabilities-and-compute methodology from 2407.21792 as a screening filter on every candidate KRI, computing each indicator's capabilities-correlation before it is allowed to gate a mitigation. (3) Replace or supplement high-correlation indicators with the low-correlation/decorrelated measures Safetywashing validates (dynamic jailbreak/GCG attacks, RMS calibration error, WMDP-style propensity measures) so that crossing a KRI threshold reflects risk-specific change. (4) Express the result as a 'KRI validity certificate' that reports each indicator's capabilities-correlation and a Brier-style decomposition, formalizing what 'appropriate access' to a meaningful KRI means.

Novelty: Neither paper connects benchmark-validity science to operational risk thresholds. Safetywashing diagnoses the measurement pathology but offers no governance mechanism; the risk-management framework builds the threshold mechanism but explicitly assumes valid risk-capability links it admits are not yet established. The combination yields a concrete, testable methodology (and a reusable audit instrument) for making frontier safety frameworks robust to safetywashing.

Feasibility and evaluation: Both inputs are computational and reproducible (lm-eval-harness; published correlation tables), so a pilot can re-score the indicator menus proposed in responsible-scaling policies and measure how many proposed KRIs would fail a decorrelation test. Success criterion: demonstrate at least one safety policy whose KRI would trip on compute alone, then show a substituted decorrelated KRI that does not.

- **Builds on:** [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2502.06656]] [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2407.21792]] [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2307.03718]]
- **Novelty:** incremental — 
- **Feasibility:** Highly feasible as a measurement pilot with one bottleneck. Both inputs are reproducible: Safetywashing ships public code and correlation tables and uses lm-eval-harness; the SaferAI KRI/KCI machinery is fully specified (2502.06656) and instantiated in public RSPs/FSFs. A team can extract indicator menus from published frameworks, map each KRI to a benchmark, compute capability/compute correlation via the existing PCA+Spearman pipeline over an open model suite, and flag indicators that cross threshold on scaling alone. The success criterion (find a KRI that trips on compute alone, then substitute a decorrelated one) is concrete and achievable with open models, no frontier access needed. Bottleneck: many real frontier KRIs are bespoke uplift/agentic/elicitation evals (CBRN, cyber, autonomy), not public benchmarks, so correlation cannot be computed without provider cooperation or proxies. Other limits: decorrelation is necessary but not sufficient for validity; correlation estimates are sample-sensitive and gameable via sandbagging; the Brier framing needs catastrophic ground-truth labels that mostly do not exist.</feasibility>

## 2. An Empirical Capture-Signature Detector for AI Safety Regulation, Built on a Coded Regulatory Corpus  _(combination, score=0.62)_
Problem and gap: 'AI safety and regulatory capture' (doi:10.1007/s00146-025-02534-0) argues AI-safety regulation strongly exemplifies capture-prone industries and predicts specific mechanisms (compliance costs not scaled to firm size so fixed costs fall on small firms, carve-outs/grandfathering, ethics-washing), but its central limitation is that it is purely predictive: it states there is no formal test or empirical instrument to detect whether an AI regulatory regime has been captured. Separately, AGORA (doi:10.1609/aies.v7i1.31615) provides a rigorously annotated, taxonomy-coded corpus of AI laws and policies (77 codes spanning governance strategies, incentives for compliance, application domains; validated inter-rater reliability), but uses it descriptively, not to test theoretical hypotheses about capture.

Combination / method: Turn the regulatory-capture theory into measurable text-derived signatures and operationalize them on AGORA's coded instruments, borrowing the quantitative correlation/validity discipline from 'Safetywashing' (doi:10.48550/arxiv.2407.21792). Concretely: (1) Derive a capture-signature codebook from 2534-0's mechanisms (size-blind fixed compliance costs, grandfathering/carve-out clauses, voluntary-vs-mandatory provisions, presence/absence of enforcement, ethics-washing language). (2) Map these onto AGORA's existing 'incentives for compliance' and 'governance strategies' codes and add new codes where needed, reusing AGORA's dual-annotation + Gwet's AC1 reliability protocol. (3) Adopt Safetywashing's posture that 'intuitive verbal arguments poorly predict empirical correlations' by statistically testing whether capture-signature density correlates with proxies for industry influence (lobbying records, firm-size provisions) rather than asserting capture rhetorically.

Novelty: This is the first attempt to convert the regulatory-capture argument into a reproducible, corpus-grounded measurement, directly addressing the gap each paper names. AGORA supplies the substrate and annotation rigor; the capture paper supplies the theory; Safetywashing supplies the empirical-validity stance that distinguishes a real signal from a plausible story.

Feasibility and evaluation: AGORA is publicly available with bulk data and a coding interface, and its corpus skews to U.S. federal instruments where lobbying disclosure data exists, enabling a concrete first study. Success criterion: produce a ranked list of AI safety instruments by capture-signature score with reported inter-rater reliability, and validate that high-signature instruments disproportionately exempt large incumbents.

- **Builds on:** [[doi:10.1007/s00146-025-02534-0]] [[doi:10.1609/aies.v7i1.31615]] [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2407.21792]]
- **Novelty:** novel — No existing work performs the specific synthesis proposed: deriving a text-derived "capture-signature" codebook from the AI-safety-capture theory (doi:10.1007/s00146-025-02534-0), operationalizing it on AGORA's coded AI-law corpus (doi:10.1609/aies.v7i1.31615) with formal IRR (Gwet AC1), and validating it Safetywashing-style against lobbying/firm-size proxies. The two closest AI-capture papers use different, weaker methods: (1) arXiv:2410.13042 "How Do AI Companies 'Fine-Tune' Policy?" relies on 17 expert interviews + targeted web searches — no text coding of law corpora, no IRR, no lobbying/firm-size correlation; (2) arXiv:2605.06806 "Big AI's Regulatory Capture" uses Design Science Research on 100 news articles to build a 27-mechanism taxonomy, reaching consensus by discussion (no quantified IRR), and explicitly notes it does NOT analyze a coded AI-law corpus, extract text-derived signatures, report IRR, or cross-reference lobbying/firm-size data. The general capture-measurement literature (Carpenter, "Detecting and Measuring Capture" in Preventing Regulatory Capture / Tobin Project) establishes standard indicators (revolving door, comment-vs-final-text drift, enforcement leniency) but is not AI-specific and not corpus-coded; it also flags the core inferential hazard that industry-aligned outcomes need not equal capture. NLP-on-regulation work exists but targets adjacent goals: RegNLP/compliance retrieval, firm-level regulatory-exposure indices, and lobbying-position extraction (arXiv:2504.15333, which does link firm size to lobbying positions but on legislative bills, not AI-law capture signatures). AGORA itself is documented as used descriptively (exploration/filtering), with no published capture-hypothesis test. The constituent methods (deductive codebook coding, IRR, signature-vs-proxy correlation) are individually mature, so the contribution is a novel application/combination rather than a new method.
- **Feasibility:** Moderately-to-highly feasible for a first study, with real measurement-validity risks. STRENGTHS: AGORA is publicly available with bulk data, full document text, an existing taxonomy (incl. "incentives for compliance" and "governance strategies" codes), a documented dual-annotation + reliability workflow, and a US-federal skew that aligns with available lobbying disclosure data (LDA filings, OpenSecrets/OpenLobby). The capture paper provides concrete, codable mechanisms (size-blind fixed compliance costs, grandfathering/carve-outs, voluntary-vs-mandatory, enforcement presence/absence, ethics-washing language). Deductive codebook coding with Gwet AC1 is standard and within reach of a small team. The phase-1 deliverable (a ranked capture-signature score per instrument with reported IRR) is concrete and achievable. KEY RISKS / ASSUMPTIONS: (1) Construct validity — the deepest problem, flagged by the capture-measurement literature itself: a text "capture signature" does not establish capture. Size-blind fixed costs or carve-outs can reflect legitimate proportionality or risk-tiering, not capture; correlation with incumbent benefit is not causal evidence of industry influence. The Safetywashing posture mitigates rhetorical overreach but cannot by itself license a causal "captured/not-captured" verdict. (2) Sample/statistical power — AGORA's binding US-federal AI-SAFETY instruments are a small subset (the archive is ~330+ instruments across all jurisdictions, many non-binding/state/foreign); the n with both a clean capture score AND matchable lobbying records may be too small for robust correlation, risking underpowered or noisy results. (3) Linkage difficulty — mapping an instrument to the relevant lobbying spend and to "firm-size provisions" requires non-trivial entity resolution and judgment about which filings target which instrument; "ethics-washing language" coding is subjective and may depress IRR. (4) Confounding — public and incumbent interests can coincide; without rulemaking-comment-to-final-text drift or revolving-door data, attributing exemptions to capture is weak. (5) Annotator burden and the need to add new codes (with re-validation) adds scope. Overall: the descriptive measurement instrument and ranked list are clearly achievable; the validation step is feasible but its inferential claims will need to be hedged to "capture-consistent signatures," not detected capture.
- **Assumptions:** 1) The 2534-0 mechanisms are reliably codable from instrument text (some, e.g. ethics-washing, are interpretive and may yield low AC1). 2) Enough binding US-federal AI-safety instruments in AGORA can be matched to lobbying/firm-size data for adequate statistical power. 3) Lobbying disclosure can be attributed to specific instruments and firms via tractable entity resolution. 4) "Disproportionately exempts large incumbents" is operationalizable from coded provisions (firm-size thresholds, grandfathering dates). 5) Reviewers/users will accept correlational evidence as "capture-consistent" rather than over-reading it as proof of capture; the project must frame claims accordingly to avoid the inferential error the capture-measurement literature warns about. 6) AGORA's license/terms permit extending its codebook and republishing derived scores.

## 3. Instrumenting the Invisible: A Mandatory Incident-Reporting Taxonomy for Distributed, Hidden Failures in Deployed AI Stacks  _(original, score=0.62)_
Open problem targeted: the hidden-safety-failures perspective (doi:10.1007/s43681-026-01132-0) argues that the most consequential deployed-system failures are 'quieter, distributed across components, and normalized by workflows', that 'AI safety is an emergent property of the deployment stack, not a scalar property of a model', and that safety should be judged by whether errors remain 'visible, contestable, containable, and recoverable' -- but it explicitly leaves this as a perspective piece with 'illustrative rather than validated controls and indicators'. In parallel, the five-layer governance paper (doi:10.1108/tg-03-2025-0065) identifies AI *incident reporting* as its starkest gap, lacking 'legal mandates, standardized taxonomies, systematic procedures, and certification mechanisms in most jurisdictions', and AGORA (doi:10.1609/aies.v7i1.31615) shows the governance corpus is skewed toward pre-deployment US instruments with thin post-deployment monitoring. New direction: design and validate a *hidden-failure incident-reporting schema* that operationalizes the four integrity criteria (visible/contestable/containable/recoverable) into mandatory, machine-readable report fields, indexed to where in the deployment stack the failure arose. The schema borrows the layered ontology of sociotechnical evaluation (capability / human-interaction / systemic-impact layers and the layer-specific responsibility assignment in doi:10.48550/arxiv.2310.11986) to fix accountability for *post-deployment* hidden failures the way that paper fixed it for pre-deployment evaluation, and it adapts the system-level (not model-level) evaluation stance of doi:10.1145/3664646.3664766 so that reports capture failures arising from component interplay and environmental affordances rather than single model outputs. The genuinely new element is making 'hidden' failures *instrumentable and reportable by construction*: each schema field is a forcing function that converts a normalized-by-workflow degradation (e.g., silent retrieval drift, eroded human-override pathways) into a contestable, logged event. Deliverable: the schema, a mapping onto AGORA's 77-code taxonomy to show which codes it newly populates, and a pilot annotation of real incidents to test whether the four-criteria fields can be reliably coded -- turning an acknowledged perspective into a validated reporting standard and giving the five-layer framework's empty 'incident reporting' layer a concrete instrument.

- **Novelty:** novel — The specific synthesis appears genuinely new, though it sits in an actively populated niche. (1) The four integrity criteria (visible/contestable/containable/recoverable) originate in the grounding paper itself (Springer, doi:10.1007/s43681-026-01132-0), which explicitly labels its controls/indicators "illustrative rather than validated" — so operationalizing them into mandatory machine-readable report fields is a clear open follow-on, not yet done. (2) Closest competitor is Agarwal & Nene, "Standardised schema and taxonomy for AI incident databases in critical digital infrastructure" (arXiv:2501.17037, IEEE 2025): a 30-field schema + taxonomy. On inspection it does NOT operationalize the four integrity criteria, does NOT index failures to sociotechnical deployment-stack layers, does NOT target hidden/silent/normalized-by-workflow degradations, and does NOT map onto AGORA's 77 codes — it is harm/severity/cause-oriented for telecom-style infrastructure. (3) Bieringer et al., "A taxonomy for reporting and describing AI security incidents" (arXiv:2412.14855) is scoped to AI *security* incidents (adversarial/disclosure), not normalized operational degradations or the integrity-criteria framing. (4) The OECD Common Reporting Framework for AI Incidents and the AI Incidents Monitor (29 criteria / 8 dimensions: metadata, harm, people-and-planet, economic) exist and are the de-facto standardization effort, but are also harm/metadata-centric, not built around hidden-failure instrumentability or the four criteria. (5) "Normalization of deviance in AI" is discussed conceptually (Embrace The Red 2025, Willison) but not turned into a reporting schema. No source was found that (a) converts the four integrity criteria into forcing-function report fields, (b) indexes them to the capability/human-interaction/systemic-impact layers of arXiv:2310.11986, and (c) cross-walks the result onto AGORA's 77-code taxonomy with a pilot inter-rater annotation. That combined artifact does not appear to exist. Relevance to AI-safety-intersect-AI-policy is high: it directly targets the post-deployment monitoring / incident-reporting gap named by two of the grounding governance papers. ## 4. KRI/KCI Threshold Pairs Transplanted to Sociotechnical Hidden-Failure Instrumentation  _(transplant, score=0.5)_
METHOD (from 'A Frontier AI Risk Management Framework', doi:10.48550/arxiv.2502.06656): The paper operationalizes an abstract risk tolerance into paired Key Risk Indicators (KRIs) and Key Control Indicators (KCIs) joined by if-then logic, with the formal three-way relationship that fixing any two of {risk tolerance, KRI threshold, KCI threshold} determines the third, plus continuous monitoring of indicators against predetermined thresholds. This machinery was designed for model-level catastrophic capabilities (bio, cyber). IDEA: Transplant the KRI/KCI formalism from the model layer to that paper's five integrity layers (e.g., overreliance/calibration, RAG retrieval integrity, prompt-injection/control integrity, persistent-memory safety, agentic tool use). For each layer, define a KRI that instruments hidden-failure precursors (e.g., rate of un-contested low-calibration outputs, fraction of agent actions lacking a recoverable rollback point) and a paired KCI that must fire when the KRI crosses threshold (e.g., human-in-the-loop escalation rate, containment coverage). Apply the three-way tolerance/KRI/KCI relationship to derive, per layer, the minimum control coverage required to keep a stated 'integrity tolerance' satisfied. WHY IT WORKS / NOVELTY: The hidden-failures paper provides the layered hazard structure but lacks quantitative thresholds; the risk-management paper provides rigorous threshold machinery but only targets dramatic model capabilities, leaving exactly the 'quiet, distributed' failures uninstrumented. The transplant turns the hidden-failures paper's illustrative control table into a validated, threshold-driven monitoring scheme and extends KRI/KCI beyond single-model catastrophic risk to deployment-stack integrity. DELIVERABLE: a per-layer KRI/KCI specification with if-then triggers and a worked derivation of required control coverage for one agentic deployment.

## 5. Mapping Sociotechnical Safety Evaluation Layers onto a Regulatory Certification Pipeline for Systemic-Impact Risks  _(combination, score=0.42)_
Problem and gap: 'Sociotechnical Safety Evaluation of Generative AI Systems' (doi:10.48550/arxiv.2310.11986) shows that capability evaluation is necessary but insufficient, proposing three layers (capability, human interaction, systemic impact) and documenting a severe 'context gap': human-interaction and systemic-impact evaluations are rare, ~75% of evaluations target text, and third-party/public actors are often best positioned for systemic-impact evaluation. 'Model evaluation for extreme risks' (doi:10.48550/arxiv.2305.15324) similarly argues evaluations must be embedded into governance (responsible training/deployment, transparency, security) via internal, external-research-access, and independent-audit sources. But neither connects these evaluation layers to a concrete regulatory conformity mechanism. Conversely, the 'Five-layer framework for AI governance' (doi:10.1108/tg-03-2025-0065) provides a regulation-to-certification pipeline (laws -> standards -> assessment procedures -> tools/metrics -> certification) with assigned ownership, but treats AI evaluation generically and identifies its own gap: missing standardized assessment procedures and certification for underdeveloped domains.

Combination / method: Cross-walk the sociotechnical evaluation taxonomy (2310.11986) and the dangerous-capability/alignment evaluation sources (2305.15324) onto the five governance layers (tg-03-2025-0065). Specifically: (1) Assign each evaluation layer a position in the certification pipeline, e.g., capability/dangerous-capability evals -> standardized assessment procedures owned by developers; human-interaction evals -> tools/metrics owned by application developers; systemic-impact evals -> certification-layer activities owned by independent third parties (matching both papers' claim that public actors are best positioned for systemic evaluation). (2) Use the five-layer framework's gap-identification case-study method to locate where systemic-impact risks lack standards, procedures, and certification today. (3) Specify the external-access modes from 2305.15324 (API research access, independent audit) as the access standard required at the certification layer.

Novelty: This is the first explicit bridge from a sociotechnical, layered evaluation science to an enforceable certification architecture, resolving the certification paper's 'generic evaluation' weakness and giving the evaluation papers the governance teeth they call for. It specifically targets the under-instrumented systemic-impact layer where evaluation responsibility and regulatory accountability are currently both diffuse.

Feasibility and evaluation: All three are conceptual frameworks with concrete taxonomies and the evaluation paper releases an open evaluation repository coded by risk area and layer, enabling a tabletop validation. Success criterion: instantiate the cross-walk for one systemic-impact risk (e.g., misinformation, the case used in 2310.11986) and show which certification-layer artifacts (standard, procedure, metric, certifier) are missing and who must own them.

- **Builds on:** [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2310.11986]] [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2305.15324]] [[doi:10.1108/tg-03-2025-0065]]
- **Novelty:** incremental — No single published work performs the exact proposed cross-walk (Weidinger 2310.11986 three sociotechnical layers + Shevlane 2305.15324 extreme-risk eval sources mapped onto the five-layer regulation->certification pipeline with ownership assignment and a per-risk artifact-gap instantiation). However, every component and most of the connective tissue already exists, so the contribution is a synthesis rather than a new idea:

1) Bench-2-CoP (arXiv 2508.05464) already maps ~195k benchmark questions onto the EU AI Act capability/propensity taxonomy and quantifies systemic-risk coverage gaps (Loss of Control 0.4%, Cyber Offence 0.8%). This is precisely an "evaluations -> regulatory taxonomy -> gap" mapping, though at the capability/benchmark level, not the full 3-layer sociotechnical taxonomy.
2) "Who Evaluates AI's Social Impacts? Mapping Coverage and Gaps in First and Third Party Evaluations" (arXiv 2511.05613) empirically maps which actor (first vs third party) covers which social/systemic-impact dimensions and finds third parties carry systemic coverage — directly substantiating (and partly pre-empting) the proposal's ownership claim that public/third-party actors own systemic-impact evaluation. It does not connect to a certification pipeline.
3) "GPAI Evaluations Standards Taskforce" (RAND, arXiv 2411.13808) proposes standardizing GPAI evaluations and housing them in EU AI Act bodies (the standards/assessment-procedure layer the idea wants to populate).
4) The five-layer governance framework itself (arXiv 2509.11332 = doi:10.1108/tg-03-2025-0065) already demonstrates its gap-identification case-study method on AI fairness and incident reporting — applying it to a third risk (misinformation/systemic impact) is a routine extension of the authors' own method, not a novel method.
5) "Navigating the sociotechnical labyrinth: Dynamic certification for responsible embodied AI" (arXiv 2409.00015) already links sociotechnical context layers to a certification process (for embodied AI), and FPF/CEN-CENELEC JTC 21 work maps conformity-assessment procedures to AI Act high-risk requirements.
6) The CETAS/Alan Turing Institute report "Realising the Potential of Sociotechnical Approaches to AI Evaluation" is a policy-facing treatment of exactly this theme (sociotechnical evaluation -> governance), suggesting the space is actively occupied.

Net: the specific titled artifact does not exist, but the load-bearing claims (systemic-impact eval gap, third-party ownership of systemic evals, eval-to-AI-Act mapping, layered certification) are each already published. This is a credible literature-synthesis / position paper, not a novel mechanism.
- **Feasibility:** High as a conceptual/desk-research contribution; low marginal effort. All three inputs are public conceptual frameworks with explicit taxonomies; 2310.11986 ships an open evaluation repository coded by risk area and layer, so the tabletop validation (instantiate the cross-walk for one systemic-impact risk such as misinformation and enumerate which certification-layer artifacts — standard, procedure, metric, certifier — are missing and who owns them) is genuinely doable by one researcher in weeks with no compute, no model access, and no data collection. The success criterion is concrete and checkable.

Key risks/assumptions that reduce value rather than feasibility: (a) the cross-walk is largely a one-to-one table-filling exercise, so the intellectual contribution rests entirely on the quality of the ownership/gap arguments, not on any empirical result; (b) the proposed ownership assignment (capability->developers, human-interaction->application developers, systemic->independent third parties) is asserted, not validated, and real conformity regimes (EU AI Act) place much capability/systemic-risk evaluation duty on GPAI providers via codes of practice with presumption-of-conformity, which partly contradicts the clean ownership split; (c) generalizing from one risk (misinformation) to the framework's correctness is weak external validity — the same critique 2310.11986 makes of single-context evaluations applies to a single-risk validation here.
- **Assumptions:** 1) The three cited frameworks are mutually commensurable enough that evaluation "layers" map cleanly onto governance "layers" — in practice the sociotechnical layers are organized by locus of harm (model/interaction/society) while the five governance layers are organized by abstraction (law/standard/procedure/tool/certification); these are orthogonal axes, so the mapping is a 3x5 matrix, not a 1:1 assignment as the proposal implies. 2) Third parties can and should own systemic-impact certification — but current law (EU AI Act GPAI regime, codes of practice) assigns much of this to providers, so the proposed ownership is normative, not descriptive. 3) A misinformation case-study generalizes to the framework. 4) The eval repository's layer coding is granular enough to populate certification-layer artifacts (it codes evaluations, not standards/certifiers, so artifact-gap identification at the certification layer requires additional manual regulatory mapping not contained in the repo). 5) "Certification" as a conformity mechanism is the right governance teeth for systemic risks, vs. alternatives like transparency reporting, audit, or deployment authorization (cf. arXiv 2601.08869).

## 6. A Decorrelation Audit for Safety Regulation: Empirically Testing Whether 'Safety' Requirements Track Capture and Capability Rather Than Risk Reduction  _(original, score=0.42)_
Open problem targeted: the regulatory-capture paper states plainly that 'no formal test exists to detect whether a regulatory regime has been captured' (doi:10.1007/s00146-025-02534-0), leaving its central thesis untestable. Separately, Safetywashing (doi:10.48550/arxiv.2407.21792) provides a concrete empirical instrument: it shows many 'safety' benchmarks are statistically redundant with general capabilities/compute and argues every safety benchmark should report its capabilities-correlation. New direction: port the Safetywashing decorrelation methodology up one level of abstraction, from benchmarks to *regulatory and corporate safety requirements*, to create the first operational test for capture and safetywashing in policy. Concretely: (1) Build a coded corpus of frontier-AI safety obligations (e.g., the standards/KRI-KCI structures in doi:10.48550/arxiv.2502.06656, the substantive standards proposed in doi:10.48550/arxiv.2307.03718, and the five-layer assessment/certification requirements in doi:10.1108/tg-03-2025-0065). (2) For each obligation, define two proxy scores: a 'risk-reduction load' (how much the requirement constrains the WMDP-style/dangerous-capability dimensions that Safetywashing found decorrelated from capability, cross-referenced to the dangerous-capability taxonomy in doi:10.48550/arxiv.2305.15324) and a 'compliance-cost asymmetry' (fixed-cost burden that, per doi:10.1007/s00146-025-02534-0, falls disproportionately on small firms because the EU AI Act 'makes no provision for firm size'). (3) Test the capture hypothesis quantitatively: a requirement is flagged as capture-prone when it imposes high fixed compliance cost but low marginal risk-reduction load and is decorrelated from the genuinely-hard-to-fake risk axes. This converts the paper's acknowledged 'contingent empirical hypothesis' into a reproducible audit metric, and gives the differential-progress framing of doi:10.48550/arxiv.2312.08039 (high ratio of safeguard progress to capabilities progress) a measurable regulatory analogue. ## 9. Zero-Knowledge Threshold Attestation: Proving KRI/KCI Compliance to Regulators Without Disclosing Model Internals or Eval Methods  _(original, score=0.31)_
Open problem targeted: the Frontier AI Risk Management Framework (doi:10.48550/arxiv.2502.06656) operationalizes risk tolerance into paired Key Risk Indicator / Key Control Indicator thresholds with if-then logic, but admits 'current quantitative risk assessment methods are insufficient to rigorously demonstrate that KCI thresholds keep risks below the risk tolerance' and that the work happens inside the developer. The dangerous-capability-evaluation literature compounds this: external evaluators 'often receive limited model access, limited information, and little time' and there is no shared framework for evaluator access (the access-taxonomy problem motivating doi:10.48550/arxiv.2305.15324's three sources of evaluations: internal, external API, external audit). Meanwhile, the 'what form' paper (doi:10.3389/fpos.2025.1561776) observes that cryptographic techniques (zero-knowledge proofs, homomorphic encryption) 'can enable privacy-preserving compliance verification' but leaves this completely unspecified and flags that they can be 'exploited for illicit anonymity'. New direction: design a *zero-knowledge threshold-attestation protocol* in which a developer cryptographically proves to a regulator the statement 'on the agreed dangerous-capability evaluation E, the measured KRI value was below threshold T and the deployed control achieved KCI >= K' WITHOUT revealing the model weights, the eval prompts (which are themselves info-hazards per the proliferation concern in doi:10.48550/arxiv.2307.03718), or the raw scores. The contribution is the missing bridge between three currently-disconnected literatures: it gives the KRI/KCI accounting of doi:10.48550/arxiv.2502.06656 a verifiable external check; it instantiates the under-specified cryptographic-compliance gesture of doi:10.3389/fpos.2025.1561776 into a concrete commitment-and-proof scheme with a trusted eval-harness commitment to prevent gaming; and it addresses the safety/security clash flagged in doi:10.48550/arxiv.2405.19524, where transparency-for-safety can leak attack surface. Deliverable: a protocol spec plus a threat model showing which of the framework's KRIs are ZK-attestable (numeric thresholds over a fixed benchmark) versus which resist attestation (open-ended red-teaming), turning 'appropriate access' from a slogan into a typed set of cryptographic guarantees.

- **Novelty:** incremental — The core cryptographic mechanism is already published. South et al. 2024, "Verifiable evaluations of machine learning models using zkSNARKs" (arXiv:2402.02675), is the first end-to-end demonstration of zkSNARK proofs that a fixed-private-weight model achieves a stated performance/fairness metric over a benchmark without revealing weights — essentially the proposal's primary deliverable. (2) Scale. zkSNARK proving of full forward passes for a 100B+-parameter frontier model across thousands of benchmark prompts is at or beyond current zkML practicality, so a real frontier-model demonstration is not yet tractable — the contribution would realistically be a spec + small-model proof-of-concept, not a deployable system.

The grounded "what form" paper (doi:10.3389/fpos.2025.1561776) already notes ZK techniques can be "exploited for illicit anonymity." A protocol that lets a developer prove KRI/KCI compliance while concealing all internals AND the benchmark could be misused to manufacture a veneer of verified safety over a gamed or sandbagged evaluation (exploiting the hidden-harness trust gap), making non-compliance harder for regulators to detect than under direct-access auditing. The work itself contains no offensive capability and no proliferation-relevant content (it argues to KEEP eval prompts hidden), so there is no info-hazard in publishing it; the concern is purely that the mechanism, if deployed naively without a trustworthy harness-commitment authority, could reduce rather than increase regulatory assurance. Surface this caveat prominently in any threat model; do not withhold.
