## 3. A Falsifiable Capture-Risk Index for AI Safety Regulation: Operationalizing the Missing 'Test for Capture'  _(original, score=0.56)_
Open problem targeted: The regulatory-capture paper (doi:10.1007/s00146-025-02534-0) explicitly states its central limitation is that 'no formal test exists to detect whether a regulatory regime has been captured' and that capture 'cannot be straightforwardly measured empirically,' leaving its argument 'largely predictive and indirect.' This is the gap. New direction: Build a falsifiable, ex-ante Capture-Risk Index (CRI) that converts the paper's qualitative capture indicators into measurable, regulation-specific signals computable BEFORE a regime matures. Concretely, the CRI scores any proposed AI-safety rule on observable proxies the paper itself names but never quantifies: (a) compliance-cost regressivity (does the rule scale obligations with firm size/revenue, or impose fixed costs that fall disproportionately on small firms? this directly operationalizes the paper's claim that the EU AI Act 'makes no provision for firm size'), (b) carve-out/grandfathering density (count and beneficiary-concentration of exemptions, thresholds, and transition provisions in the legal text), (c) revolving-door and consultation-asymmetry metrics (share of comment-period input and advisory-body seats held by incumbents vs. marginalized stakeholders, operationalizing the 'democratic deficit' and 'industry has far more resources to participate' claims), and (d) standard-setting dependence on incumbent-controlled bodies. Method: Assemble a labeled corpus of real AI rules and standards (EU AI Act, GPAI Code of Practice, NIST AI RMF, US executive actions) plus historical captured/uncaptured regimes from adjacent sectors named in the verified corpus (the frontier-AI paper's nuclear/aviation analogies; the safety-vs-security paper's NIST/DARPA comparisons), extract the CRI features via legal-text NLP plus public lobbying/consultation records, and validate against ex-post capture outcomes in those mature sectors to calibrate thresholds. Crucially, design the index so each proposed AI rule yields a pre-deployment capture-risk score with explicit falsification conditions (e.g., 'if regressivity > X and carve-out concentration > Y, predict oligopoly entrenchment within N years'), turning an untestable warning into a monitorable instrument. Why genuinely new: the source paper critiques all three existing mitigations (regulatory markets, open ecosystems, transparency) as insufficient and offers no measurement tool; the CRI is a fourth, complementary, quantitative early-warning layer that policymakers can run on draft text. Builds on the frontier-regulation paper's compute-threshold and licensing analysis (doi:10.48550/arxiv.2307.03718) because its own proposed instruments are exactly the kind of fixed-cost rules the CRI would flag for capture risk.

- **Builds on:** [[doi:10.1007/s00146-025-02534-0]] [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2307.03718]]
- **Novelty:** novel — The specific artifact proposed — a composite, ex-ante, RULE-SPECIFIC capture-risk index extracted from draft AI legal text via NLP, fused with consultation/revolving-door data, calibrated against ex-post outcomes in analog sectors, and emitting explicit falsification thresholds — does not appear to exist. However, every ingredient exists independently, so this is novelty-by-recombination rather than a fundamentally new construct:
(1) Revolving Door Indicator/Index already operationalizes door metrics (Brezis 2014; finance-sector 20-30% transition rates). (2) Carpenter & Moss, 'Preventing Regulatory Capture' includes a dedicated 'Detecting and Measuring Capture' chapter (Cambridge) — the canonical prior framework. (3) The two most relevant AI-specific papers are BOTH qualitative and explicitly retrospective/descriptive, and explicitly disclaim quantification: arXiv:2410.13042 ('How Do AI Companies Fine-Tune Policy?') builds a 15-mechanism taxonomy from 17 expert interviews and states capture 'is difficult to measure quantitatively'; arXiv:2605.06806 ('Big AI's Regulatory Capture') builds a 27-mechanism taxonomy by annotating ~100 news articles and states it is 'primarily descriptive and interpretive' and does not 'quantify the extent to which capture occurs' — neither produces an ex-ante, text-derived, rule-specific predictive score with falsification conditions. (4) OECD iREG composite indicators measure stakeholder engagement/RIA but at COUNTRY level, not rule-level capture risk. (5) Legislative-text NLP for burden/restrictiveness exists (RegBR; John Nay, 'NLP and ML for Law and Policy Texts'), but is not applied to capture-proxy extraction. (6) EU AI Act SME compliance-cost regressivity is heavily documented (setup ~EUR 193k-330k; 5-7% of budget for SMEs vs 2-3% for large firms). (7) HHI/CR4 and lobbying-density/comment-asymmetry are established capture/concentration proxies. The source paper's stated gap ('no formal test exists to detect whether a regulatory regime has been captured') is real and genuinely unaddressed by an ex-ante, draft-text-scoring instrument. The contribution is a credible synthesis, not a new measurement primitive.
- **Feasibility:** Mixed — strong on the descriptive index, weak on the causal/predictive validation that is the idea's headline promise.
TRACTABLE parts: (a) The legal-text feature extraction (carve-out/grandfathering density, threshold counts, firm-size-scaling of obligations) is straightforward NLP/annotation on a small, finite corpus (EU AI Act, GPAI CoP, NIST AI RMF, US EOs) — could be done largely by hand plus LLM-assisted extraction; the corpus is small enough that manual coding is realistic. (b) Compliance-cost regressivity and consultation/advisory-seat asymmetry are computable from public RIA estimates and consultation registers/transparency registers. (c) Revolving-door data exists for some jurisdictions. This part yields a usable descriptive scorecard quickly.
WEAK parts (the crux): (1) VALIDATION HAS NO GROUND TRUTH. 'Captured vs. uncaptured' regimes are contested labels, not observable facts; capture is latent and definitionally disputed, so any labeled corpus encodes the labeler's priors — circular validation risk. (2) TINY N. Calibrating thresholds (regressivity > X, carve-out concentration > Y) requires many independent mature regimes with known outcomes; nuclear/aviation/finance give a handful of confounded cases, far too few to fit non-arbitrary cutoffs. (3) The falsification condition ('predict oligopoly entrenchment within N years') is on a decade+ horizon, confounded by technology shocks, antitrust action, and global competition — practically unfalsifiable on policy-relevant timescales, undercutting the central 'falsifiable' selling point. (4) CONSTRUCT VALIDITY / EXPERTISE-VS-CAPTURE problem: high incumbent consultation share and fixed costs can reflect legitimate technical necessity (information asymmetry), not capture — the index cannot separate these without instruments. (5) Aggregation/weighting of the four dimensions into one score is arbitrary absent calibration data, inviting cherry-picking. Recommendation: reframe deliverable as a transparent, falsification-oriented capture-RISK SCORECARD with per-dimension scores and documented assumptions, treat thresholds as hypotheses not validated predictions, and downscope the cross-sector causal calibration to an illustrative case study rather than a calibrated predictive model.
- **Assumptions:** Key assumptions the idea rests on, ranked by fragility: (1) [FRAGILE] That ex-post capture outcomes are observable/labelable cleanly enough to calibrate thresholds — contested in the literature; capture is latent. (2) [FRAGILE] That a handful of analog mature sectors (nuclear, aviation, finance) provide enough comparable, low-confounding cases to fit numeric cutoffs that transfer to AI — strong external-validity assumption. (3) [MODERATE] That text-observable proxies (carve-out density, firm-size scaling) are valid signals of capture rather than of legitimate risk-tiering or technical necessity. (4) [MODERATE] That fixed compliance costs falling on small firms indicate capture risk rather than unavoidable safety floors — the index must distinguish regressivity-by-capture from regressivity-by-physics. (5) [MODERATE] That consultation/revolving-door records are complete and comparable across jurisdictions (EU transparency register vs US notice-and-comment vs standards bodies differ structurally). (6) [REASONABLE] That draft legal text is available pre-deployment for scoring (true for most named instruments). (7) [REASONABLE] That policymakers would actually run such a tool on their own draft rules (adoption/incentive assumption — the captured-party may have no incentive to publish a high score).
- **⚠ Dual-use note:** Low-severity, flag-only (not an info-hazard, no capability uplift). Two governance-misuse vectors worth noting, not withholding: (1) GAMING / Goodhart's law — once the index's scored features are public, sophisticated incumbents can draft rules that minimize the visible CRI score (e.g., add token SME carve-outs, pad consultation rosters with nominal small-stakeholder seats) while preserving substantive capture, turning the early-warning tool into a compliance-laundering checklist. (2) WEAPONIZATION AS A DEREGULATORY CUDGEL — because nearly any meaningful safety rule imposes some fixed cost and relies on incumbent technical input (genuine information asymmetry), a high CRI can be manufactured for almost any safety regulation and used by anti-regulation actors to brand legitimate AI-safety rules as 'captured,' chilling necessary regulation. The expertise-vs-capture conflation makes this especially exploitable. Mitigation: publish the index with explicit construct-validity caveats, require qualitative adjudication alongside the numeric score, and resist presenting the score as a verdict rather than a flag. No safety-relevant capability information is created or disclosed by this work.

## 4. Instrumenting Hidden Failures as a Security Discipline: A Safety-Security Threat Model for Distributed, Quiet AI Failures  _(combination, score=0.52)_
Combine the five-layer integrity taxonomy and 'hidden failures' framing from 'The safety failures we are not instrumenting' with the safety-vs-security reference framework (four dimensions: objectives, threat models, problem framing, governance/liability; plus the CIA-triad taxonomy) from 'AI Risk Management Should Incorporate Both Safety and Security.' The hidden-failures paper argues that the most consequential deployed-system failures are quiet, distributed across the deployment stack (RAG, prompt injection, agentic tool use, persistent memory), and degrade correction mechanisms over time -- but it treats these largely as non-adversarial integrity erosion. The safety-security paper argues that many such control-integrity problems (prompt injection, compromised security properties cascading into harmful behavior) are inherently adversarial min-max games requiring a security mindset that is routinely marginalized. The combined project would (1) re-classify each layer of the hidden-failures integrity taxonomy as predominantly safety- or security-governed, and identify the layers where the two clash (e.g., adding adversarial robustness controls that reduce benign usability); (2) extend the hidden-failures 'practical control table' of instrumentation indicators into a dual-objective monitoring framework that emits separate non-adversarial and adversarial signals per layer, operationalizing the safety-security paper's recommendation to evaluate against both threat models separately; and (3) map the result onto governance/liability so regulators can require instrumentation for the currently 'uninstrumented' failures. Output: a deployment-stack monitoring spec that makes quiet safety-and-security failures visible, contestable, and recoverable.

- **Builds on:** [[doi:10.1007/s43681-026-01132-0]] [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2405.19524]]
- **Novelty:** incremental — covered above
- **Feasibility:** High feasibility as a conceptual/governance synthesis paper, low-to-medium as anything empirically validated. (1) Steps 1 and 3 (per-layer safety/security re-classification + governance/liability mapping) are pure conceptual desk work directly composable from the two source papers — readily doable, this is the natural form of the output. (2) Step 2 (dual-signal monitoring spec) is also writable on paper, since most indicators already exist in Table 1 and only need to be bisected into adversarial vs non-adversarial channels and given attribution rules. (3) The hard, unaddressed part is operational validity: deciding whether an observed failure signal is adversarial or accidental is exactly the "gray area" Qi et al. flag (a casual user can produce adversarial-looking inputs without intent), so a clean per-layer adversarial/non-adversarial signal split is partly ill-posed and may not be empirically separable without intent attribution that monitoring cannot reliably recover. (4) Several layers (organizational integrity = fictional oversight, ecosystem integrity = model/retrieval collapse) do not map cleanly onto the security CIA-triad framing — they are sociotechnical/non-adversarial and the security half of the dual signal is weak or undefined there, so the "dual-objective per layer" promise will be uneven across the five layers. (5) Empirical validation (does the spec actually improve detection/contestability/recoverability) would require a deployed agentic stack with fault injection — substantial engineering, out of scope for the proposed analytical output.
- **Assumptions:** 1. Both grounding papers were read in full locally (work/fulltext/doi_10.1007_s43681-026-01132-0.md and doi_10.48550_arxiv.2405.19524.md). 2. The proposed output is an analytical/governance spec (paper + monitoring framework), not a validated empirical system; scored on that basis. 3. ## 6. Applying the IAEA Three-Pillar Transfer Framework to Cyber-Style Safety/Security Standard-Setting  _(transplant, score=0.42)_
METHOD transplanted: the three-pillar cross-domain transfer assessment framework — regulatory fitness, technical integration feasibility, regulatory efficacy — used in 'Towards an international regulatory framework for AI safety: lessons from the IAEA' (doi:10.1057/s41599-024-03017-1) to systematically vet whether a mature high-risk regime's mechanisms can be ported to AI. PROBLEM/DOMAIN it is applied to: the unresolved safety-vs-security tension and min-max/arms-race dynamics articulated in 'AI Risk Management Should Incorporate Both Safety and Security' (doi:10.48550/arxiv.2405.19524). The transplant: instead of borrowing from the IAEA's accident-and-safety-standard model (which the nuclear paper itself concedes fits non-adversarial risks), apply the SAME three-pillar transfer methodology to a source domain whose regime was built for adversarial min-max threats — e.g., cybersecurity vulnerability-disclosure / CVE coordination and red-team certification. The security paper argues safety has historically assumed a stable distribution while security is an inherently dynamic min-max game, and that the two can technically clash (adversarial training vs. benign accuracy). Use the three pillars to assess which cyber-regime mechanisms (coordinated disclosure, arms-race-aware continuous re-certification, separate evaluation against adversarial vs. non-adversarial threat models) can be transferred into international AI standards that hold BOTH objectives simultaneously. Novelty: combines one paper's vetted analogical-transfer methodology with another paper's diagnosis that current AI risk frameworks under-instrument adversarial security, producing a structured proposal for a safety-and-security-balanced international standard rather than a nuclear-style safety-only one.

- **Builds on:** [[doi:10.1057/s41599-024-03017-1]] [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2405.19524]]
- **Novelty:** incremental — No single work executes the exact transplant proposed (apply the IAEA paper's specific three-pillar transfer methodology — regulatory fitness, technical integration feasibility, regulatory efficacy — to a cybersecurity source domain, explicitly motivated by the 2405.19524 safety-vs-security min-max diagnosis, to design a dual-objective international standard). However, every component is already well-developed in the literature, leaving little conceptual headroom:

(1) Cyber→AI mechanism transfer is already being done WITHOUT the formal three-pillar wrapper: 'From Firewalls to Frontiers: AI Red-Teaming is a Domain-Specific Evolution of Cyber Red-Teaming' (arXiv 2509.11398) explicitly ports cyber red-teaming, coordinated vulnerability disclosure (CVD), continuous monitoring, and certification culture to AI, and diagnoses where they fail to transfer — essentially the proposal's payload minus the named framework. 'Coordinated Flaw Disclosure for AI: Beyond Security Vulnerabilities' (arXiv 2402.07039) and CMU/SEI + CERT/CC work port CVD/CVE to AI directly. CISA's JCDC AI Cybersecurity Collaboration Playbook operationalizes coordinated disclosure for AI in practice.

(2) The source-domain choice debate (nuclear vs. cybersecurity for adversarial/min-max AI risks) is already a recognized research thread: 'The Nuclear Analogy in AI Governance Research' (arXiv 2510.21203, surveying 43 works) and CSET/AI-Frontiers pieces ('Nuclear Non-Proliferation Is the Wrong Framework for AI Governance') argue cybersecurity/enterprise risk regimes are better fits than nuclear for AI's general-purpose, adversarial character — the proposal's core motivating claim is thus established prior art.

(3) The safety+security dual-objective standard-setting goal is being pursued by AISI/standards literature (arXiv 2409.11314 on AISIs and international standards; ISO/IEC 42001 + EU AI Act pairing with continuous monitoring and adversarial-testing obligations for systemic-risk models; arXiv 2503.16861 on third-party flaw disclosure).

The genuine gap is methodological packaging: nobody has run the IAEA paper's named three-pillar VETTING instrument on a cyber source regime as a structured comparison against the nuclear transplant. That is a real but modest contribution — a structured re-derivation of conclusions the field is already reaching by other means. Hence 'incremental,' not 'novel.'
- **Feasibility:** Highly feasible as a conceptual/policy-analysis paper; this is desk research requiring no compute, data, or model access. The two grounding documents supply both the method (three-pillar transfer assessment) and the problem framing (safety-vs-security min-max tension), and the cyber source-domain mechanisms (CVD, CVE/CVSS scoring, red-team certification, continuous re-certification under NIS2/Cyber Resilience Act) are richly documented and already partially mapped to AI. A competent governance researcher could produce it in weeks. Main feasibility risks are intellectual, not logistical: (a) the original three-pillar framework's exact operational definitions must be extracted from the Nature paper (currently behind an auth wall — I could not verify the precise pillar definitions, only that a structured transfer-limitation analysis exists), so there is some risk the 'method' is less crisply formalized/portable than the proposal assumes; (b) the cyber regime's transfer-blockers are non-trivial — AI red-teaming tests fundamentally different properties than penetration testing (model robustness, emergent behavior), so 'technical integration feasibility' may largely conclude that mechanisms need heavy adaptation rather than clean transfer, a result already reported by arXiv 2509.11398; (c) the safety-vs-security technical clash (adversarial robustness vs. benign accuracy) is a model-property finding that does not straightforwardly translate into a standard-setting mechanism, so bridging the micro (training) and macro (international standard) levels is the hard, under-specified part. Tractability is high; depth of novel insight is the binding constraint.
- **Assumptions:** 1) That the IAEA paper's three pillars are crisply operationalized enough to be reused as a portable vetting instrument on a different source domain (unverified — Nature full text was inaccessible; only the limitations/caveat framing was confirmable). 2) That the IAEA paper actually concedes its model fits non-adversarial risks but not adversarial ones in the specific terms described — the abstract confirms it acknowledges 'distinct characteristics and risks' limit direct transfer, but the precise adversarial-vs-accident framing attributed to it should be verified against the full text. 3) That a cybersecurity governance regime is coherent enough to treat as a single 'source domain' (in reality CVD, CVE/CVSS, red-team certification, and NIS2/CRA mandates are heterogeneous instruments with different maturity and governance owners). 4) That the gap between a model-level technical finding (adversarial training degrades benign accuracy) and an international standard-setting mechanism can be bridged within one framework. 5) That an international standard can meaningfully 'hold both objectives simultaneously' given the paper's own diagnosis that they can technically clash — the proposal asserts this is achievable but the grounding paper arguably implies tradeoff management, not simultaneous satisfaction.

## 7. Capture-Resistant Frontier Evaluation Standards: Designing Dangerous-Capability Eval Regimes That Resist Regulatory Capture  _(combination, score=0.42)_
Combine the regulatory building blocks of Anderljung et al.'s 'Frontier AI Regulation' (standard-setting, registration/reporting, compliance via licensing) with Mökander-style economic analysis of regulatory capture from 'AI safety and regulatory capture' to design a frontier-AI evaluation regime that is explicitly engineered to resist capture. The capture paper shows that AI-safety regulation strongly exemplifies capture-prone industries and that fixed compliance costs (e.g., mandatory pre-deployment dangerous-capability evaluations) fall disproportionately on small firms, seeding oligopoly; the frontier-regulation paper proposes exactly such pre-deployment risk assessments, external scrutiny, and licensing without provisioning for firm size. The combined research program would (1) build a cost model of the frontier-regulation paper's proposed compliance stack (risk assessments, external expert scrutiny, post-deployment monitoring, licensing) and quantify per-firm burden across firm sizes; (2) identify which specific building blocks are most capture-prone (e.g., who controls standard-setting, whether incumbents define 'dangerous capability' thresholds, the compute-threshold definition itself); and (3) propose concrete anti-capture design patterns -- size-tiered compliance, independent/publicly-funded evaluators rather than industry-aligned 'regulatory markets', sunset clauses against rule-multiplication, and transparency-plus-enforcement -- and evaluate them against the three mitigation proposals the capture paper critiques as insufficient. Output: a capture-resistance scorecard for frontier-AI standard-setting that policymakers can apply to draft regimes (e.g., the EU GPAI Code of Practice).

## 8. Instrumenting the Safety/Security Trade-off Frontier: Pareto Risk Cards for AI Regulation  _(original, score=0.42)_
Open problem targeted: Two verified papers together expose an uninstrumented gap. The safety-vs-security paper (doi:10.48550/arxiv.2405.19524) shows safety and security can technically CLASH (e.g., adversarial training and randomized smoothing improve worst-case robustness/security but sacrifice benign accuracy, making models less safe to use), that safety is risk-minimization over a stable distribution while security is a dynamic min-max arms race, and laments that 'security mindsets are marginalized' (299 'safety' vs 5 'security' mentions in a major model report) — yet it provides only non-overlapping taxonomies, no instrument to manage the trade-off. Separately, the hidden-failures paper (doi:10.1007/s43681-026-01132-0) argues 'AI safety is an emergent property of the deployment stack, not a scalar property of a model' and that consequential failures are 'distributed, temporally extended,' but its proposed controls are admittedly 'illustrative rather than validated.' New direction: Develop 'Pareto Risk Cards' — a measurement-and-disclosure instrument that makes the safety/security trade-off a first-class, regulable object. For a given model+deployment-stack, empirically trace the Pareto frontier between safety metrics (benign-task reliability, calibration, harm-emission rate under the natural distribution) and security metrics (worst-case robustness under the min-max adversarial threat model), so that any chosen mitigation (e.g., a given adversarial-training strength) is reported as a point on a measured trade-off curve rather than a binary 'safe/secure' claim. Method: (1) define paired safety/security metrics per the source paper's four-dimension framework (objectives, threat model, problem framing, governance); (2) sweep mitigation strengths to estimate the frontier on representative agentic/RAG deployment configurations drawn from the hidden-failures taxonomy (prompt-injection control integrity, tool-use, persistent memory); (3) instrument the frontier with the hidden-failures paper's 'visible/contestable/containable/recoverable' criteria so each frontier point carries recoverability indicators, not just a scalar; (4) translate frontier disclosure into a policy artifact — a standardized Risk Card a developer must file, exposing exactly where on the safety↔security curve they chose to sit and which deployment-stack layer absorbs the residual risk. Why genuinely new: existing risk frameworks (the source paper, NIST RMF it cites) treat safety and security as parallel checklists; no work instruments their measured tension as a frontier and ties the frontier to stack-level recoverability. This directly answers both papers' stated limitations: it makes the clash measurable (answering paper 1's lack of an instrument) and replaces 'illustrative controls' with validated, frontier-grounded indicators (answering paper 2).

4. Step 4 (regulatory filing) faces the trade-secret tension flagged in current transparency-law commentary; a frontier sweep can leak mitigation internals, and gaming (reporting a flattering operating point) is hard to audit without a standardized, adversary-current benchmark.
Net: a scoped empirical demonstration on one config is feasible and publishable; the full 'regulable, validated, generalizable instrument' claim is over-promised relative to the stationarity and validation problems.
