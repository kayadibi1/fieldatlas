# The State of the Field: AI Safety ∩ AI Policy

## Scope and Reading of the Corpus

This synthesis covers the intersection of AI safety and AI policy as represented by a 38-document report corpus spanning 2020–2026, of which 21 documents were independently verified at the claim level. The wider bibliographic index (the year histogram) shows the field's explosive recent growth: from 33 indexed works in 2018 to 220 in 2024, 243 in 2025, and 174 already attributed to 2026 — roughly two-thirds of the literature is from 2023 onward. The report corpus itself is concentrated in this same window, with a cluster of foundational "primer" and landscape pieces from 2020–2022 and a dense layer of governance, evaluation, and risk-management work from 2023–2026.

The eight thematic clusters supplied with the corpus are large and overlapping, but they resolve into four substantive sub-areas that organize the discussion below: (1) **technical AI safety** (robustness, interpretability, specification, evaluation); (2) **AI governance and regulation**; (3) **AI risk management** as an engineering/organizational discipline; and (4) **alignment, ethics, and existential/long-term risk**. A recurring cross-cutting theme — the contested definition of "AI safety" itself — runs through all four.

## 1. Sub-Area: Technical AI Safety — Robustness, Assurance, Specification, Interpretability

The most settled conceptual scaffolding in the corpus comes from the CSET "Key Concepts in AI Safety" series, which partitions technical AI safety into three problem categories: **robustness** (operating safely in unfamiliar settings), **assurance** (being analyzable/understandable by human operators), and **specification** (behaving in accordance with designer intent) [[doi:10.51593/20190040]]. The series then elaborates two of these: interpretability/explainability as the route to assurance [[doi:10.51593/20190042]], and specification as the design of goals and proxy objectives [[doi:10.51593/20210031]]. A central established claim is that, unlike traditional engineered systems, modern ML methods "do not come with safety guarantees," so they can fail on previously unseen inputs [[doi:10.51593/20190040]]; misspecification is not hypothetical but already observed (e.g., engagement-optimizing recommender systems) [[doi:10.51593/20190040]]. This three-part framing is corroborated by parallel safety-standards work arguing that AI's statistical, black-box character gives "no clear cause-and-effect relationship or guarantee against unexpected... errors" and that existing software safety standards are unlikely to suffice [[doi:10.56094/jss.v55i3.39]].

**Interpretability** is treated as necessary but immature. The corpus establishes that the "black box" label is imprecise — researchers can see all parameter values; the difficulty is connecting millions or billions of weights to human-meaningful concepts [[doi:10.51593/20190042]]. Existing methods (saliency maps, feature visualization) "provide only one angle rather than a holistic view," are fallible, and lack a common vocabulary, making progress hard to measure; the pragmatic fallback is to prefer inherently interpretable models where possible [[doi:10.51593/20190042]]. Bibliometric work confirms interpretability as a genuine research cluster (with the EU a close second to the US, possibly linked to GDPR's right-to-explanation), alongside robustness (dominated by adversarial examples) and reward learning — and estimates that AI safety is "less than 1 percent of AI research overall" [[doi:10.51593/20210026]].

**What is established here:** the robustness/assurance/specification taxonomy as a shared vocabulary; the centrality and current inadequacy of interpretability; and the empirical observation that technical safety is a small, US-led, but rapidly growing fraction of AI research [[doi:10.51593/20210026]], [[doi:10.23919/mipro48935.2020.9245153]].

## 2. Sub-Area: AI Governance and Regulation

This is the densest sub-area in the policy half of the corpus. Several lines of work are convergent.

**Frontier-AI regulation as a distinct object.** A foundational claim is that "frontier AI" — highly capable foundation models — poses three distinctive regulatory problems: dangerous capabilities can arise *unexpectedly* and undetected; deployed models are hard to *control*; and capabilities *proliferate* rapidly (open-sourcing, reproduction, theft) [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2307.03718]]. The proposed response is three regulatory "building blocks" — standard-setting, registration/reporting for regulatory visibility, and compliance mechanisms (from self-certification through licensing) — with a compute (FLOP) threshold offered as a crude ex ante trigger [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2307.03718]]. A complementary "what form should it take?" analysis argues current efforts are "fragmented and reactive" and pushes a security-first, risk-based, decentralised blueprint, partly because "most countries do not follow EU ideals" [[doi:10.3389/fpos.2025.1561776]].

**Layered and structured governance frameworks** recur as a methodological signature. Proposals include a five-layer stack from regulation → standards → assessment procedures → tools/metrics → certification, validated on AI fairness (mature) versus AI incident reporting (underdeveloped) [[doi:10.1108/tg-03-2025-0065]]; a "pre-execution control" architecture borrowing from high-risk physical systems [[doi:10.2139/ssrn.6506258]]; an adaptive-hybrid governance framework linking data, algorithm, technology, and geopolitics [[doi:10.1080/09700161.2023.2288994]]; and end-to-end governance-risk assessment distinguishing "bottom-up" (model) from "top-down" (organizational) risks [[doi:10.4230/oasics.saia.2024.4]]. The trustworthy-AI tradition supplies the canonical three-pillar / seven-requirement structure (lawful, ethical, robust) and the EU AI Act's risk-based, usage-focused regulation, introducing the narrower notion of a "responsible AI system" grounded in auditability and accountability, with regulatory sandboxes as the test environment [[doi:10.1016/j.inffus.2023.101896]].

**Interoperability and international coordination** form a second axis. The OECD argues for "common guideposts" to make national AI risk-management approaches interoperable and reduce fragmentation [[doi:10.1787/ba602d18-en]]. A cross-regional study compares EU/US/UK/China risk-management strategies and how each classifies risk and structures oversight [[doi:10.1109/i-coste68047.2025.11467397]]. The most developed analogy argues that the IAEA's nuclear-safety model — standardized criteria, a neutral monitoring body, accident-response drills, an information-sharing platform — offers transferable lessons, while cautioning that nuclear and AI differ enough that an "independent, AI-specific" regime is ultimately needed [[doi:10.1057/s41599-024-03017-1]]. Infrastructure for *studying* governance is itself a contribution: AGORA compiles and codes AI laws/policies against a 77-code taxonomy of risks, harms, strategies, incentives, and domains — the "most systematic effort to date" to map the regulatory landscape, though heavily US-skewed (140 of 168 annotated instruments) [[doi:10.1609/aies.v7i1.31615]].

**What is established here:** that frontier models present a qualitatively distinct regulatory challenge tied to unexpected capabilities, control, and proliferation [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2307.03718]]; that current governance is fragmented and needs structured, multi-layer, multi-actor coordination [[doi:10.1108/tg-03-2025-0065]], [[doi:10.3389/fpos.2025.1561776]]; and that international interoperability — not a single global regulator — is the realistic near-term goal [[doi:10.1787/ba602d18-en]], [[doi:10.1057/s41599-024-03017-1]].

## 3. Sub-Area: AI Risk Management as an Engineering Discipline

A distinct cluster treats AI safety as a problem of importing mature risk-management practice (aviation, nuclear) into AI development.

The flagship contribution argues that current AI-company safety frameworks "lack the systematic rigor found in other high-risk industries," specifically missing well-defined risk tolerance, quantitative risk assessment, and systematic risk identification [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2502.06656]]. It proposes a four-component framework (identification, analysis/evaluation, treatment, governance) operationalizing a quantitative **risk tolerance** into paired **Key Risk Indicator / Key Control Indicator** thresholds via if-then logic, and notes a three-way determinacy among tolerance, KRI, and KCI thresholds [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2502.06656]]. The "should incorporate both safety and security" position sharpens the discipline's boundaries: safety concerns harm a system inflicts on its environment (largely accidental, modeled as risk minimization over a stable distribution), whereas security concerns protecting the system from adversaries (a dynamic min-max arms race over the CIA triad) — and the two can technically clash, e.g., adversarial training improving worst-case robustness while reducing benign accuracy [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2405.19524]]. This safety/security distinction is independently emphasized as "often misunderstood but crucial for responsible AI deployment" [[doi:10.34190/icair.4.1.3142]]. Auditing is positioned as a practical risk-management tool for LLMs [[doi:10.54941/ahfe1006101]], and a control-accountability theory argues risk mitigation hinges on aligning *control* and *accountability* across developers and users, a problem aggravated by AI's autonomous adaptivity [[doi:10.5465/amr.2023.0117]].

**What is established here:** that AI risk management is real but immature relative to established high-risk industries; that safety and security are distinct objectives that must both be accounted for and can trade off [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2405.19524]]; and that quantitative risk tolerance is the field's "key missing element," with developers urged to set it proactively absent regulation [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2502.06656]].

## 4. Sub-Area: Alignment, Ethics, and Long-Term / Existential Risk

The alignment cluster ranges from technical proposals to conceptual taxonomy and policy.

**Conceptual disentanglement** is a major thread. One taxonomy argues alignment is inherently multidimensional, structured along three orthogonal axes — *aim* (safety, ethicality, legality, user intent), *scope* (outcome vs. execution), and *constituency* (individual vs. collective) — and establishes the sharp claims that "safety does not entail ethicality" (a reliably functioning system controlled by terrorists is safe but unethical) while "ethicality practically implies safety" [[doi:10.1007/978-3-032-01377-4_8]]. A comprehensive alignment review frames the work as a "forward/backward alignment cycle" responding to rising misalignment risk [[doi:10.1142/s021800142539001x]], and a boundary analysis attempts to map alignment limits onto cognitive-task complexity, locating "safe operation ranges" beyond which the technology is framed as existential [[doi:10.22541/au.171697103.39692698/v1]]. **Agentic AI** is flagged as the next frontier: autonomous, goal-pursuing systems raise unintended optimization, deceptive alignment, and value drift, with technical hurdles in reward modeling, scalable oversight, and corrigibility [[doi:10.1093/oxfordhb/9780198940272.013.0006]].

**Policy-aware alignment.** A distinctive "safeguarding the safeguards" argument warns that well-intentioned alignment work can be *counterproductive*, identifying four failure modes — the relative-progress problem (capabilities outrunning safeguards), the false-sense-of-security problem, the "dangerous valley" (intermediate safety that incentivizes premature deployment), and the bad-principal problem (better alignment empowering a malicious principal) — and recommends that public-interest funders back specifically *risk-reducing* alignment [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2312.08039]]. Notably, this paper concedes that the deceptive/scheming-misalignment threat model "is acknowledged to be currently hypothetical."

**Long-term and existential framing** persists as its own cluster, connecting AI safety to superintelligence, futures studies, and existential risk, though much of it sits in the unverified portion of the corpus.

## Key Methods Across the Field

The corpus is methodologically dominated by **conceptual, position, and framework papers** — taxonomies, primers, and "building blocks" rather than experiments (e.g., [[doi:10.51593/20190040]], [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2307.03718]], [[doi:10.1145/3664646.3664766]], [[doi:10.1007/978-3-032-01377-4_8]]). Recurring concrete methods include:

- **Model evaluation / evals** as the central technical-governance bridge: splitting evals into *dangerous-capability* and *alignment* evaluations, embedding results into responsible training, deployment, transparency, and security decisions, and sourcing them internally, via API research access, and via independent audit [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2305.15324]]. A taxonomy of *access methods* for external dangerous-capability evaluation operationalizes the EU GPAI Code of Practice's vague "appropriate access" [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2601.11916]].
- **Sociotechnical / system-level evaluation**: a three-layer framework (capability → human interaction → systemic impact) grounded in system-safety engineering, with an empirical landscape review finding ~75% of evaluations target text and essentially none target video — documenting coverage, context, and multimodal gaps [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2310.11986]]. System-level (not model-centric) evaluation and terminology harmonization across AI/SE/governance communities is a parallel methodological demand [[doi:10.1145/3664646.3664766]].
- **Meta-analysis / quantitative benchmarking**: PCA and Spearman-correlation analysis of safety benchmarks against capabilities and training compute [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2407.21792]].
- **Bibliometric/science-mapping** (citation clustering, country trend plots) [[doi:10.51593/20210026]], [[doi:10.23919/mipro48935.2020.9245153]].
- **Comparative/analogical policy analysis** (nuclear/IAEA, aviation, cross-regional EU/US/UK/China) [[doi:10.1057/s41599-024-03017-1]], [[doi:10.1109/i-coste68047.2025.11467397]], [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2405.19524]].
- **Dataset construction with inter-rater reliability** as governance infrastructure [[doi:10.1609/aies.v7i1.31615]].
- **Probabilistic risk assessment** (event/fault trees, KRI/KCI thresholds, expert/Delphi elicitation) imported from high-reliability industries [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2502.06656]].

## Tensions and Debates

1. **What "AI safety" even means.** The field is repeatedly described as "poorly defined and inconsistently measured" [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2407.21792]], with deep disagreement over terminology, ontologies, and priorities across the diverse communities that claim it [[doi:10.1109/dsn-w50199.2020.00023]]. Divergent definitions of "evaluation," "testing," and "assessment" across AI, software-engineering, and governance communities actively impede unified practice [[doi:10.1145/3664646.3664766]].

2. **Safety measurement vs. capabilities.** "Safetywashing" is the field's sharpest empirical critique: many safety benchmarks are highly correlated with general capabilities and compute (e.g., preference benchmarks, certain scalable-oversight and truthfulness benchmarks), so capability gains can be misrepresented as safety progress — whereas weaponization (WMDP) and many bias benchmarks are *not* so correlated [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2407.21792]]. Crucially, "increasing capabilities does not necessarily make AI safer" [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2407.21792]].

3. **Visible vs. hidden failures.** A reframing argues the field over-focuses on dramatic, visible harms while "the most consequential failures are quieter" — distributed, temporally extended, normalized by workflows — and that "AI safety is an emergent property of the deployment stack, not a scalar property of a model," to be judged by whether errors remain visible, contestable, containable, and recoverable [[doi:10.1007/s43681-026-01132-0]]. This echoes the socio-technical reframing that current safety vocabulary captures only part of real-world incidents [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2401.10899]].

4. **Safety vs. security; safety vs. ethicality.** Safety and security can technically clash (robustness vs. accuracy) [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2405.19524]], and safety is conceptually decoupled from ethicality — a safe system can be unethical [[doi:10.1007/978-3-032-01377-4_8]].

5. **Regulation as cure vs. regulation as risk.** Against the pro-regulation consensus, a prominent argument holds that AI-safety regulation "strongly exemplifies the features of industries that are highly subject to capture," that the EU AI Act's lack of firm-size provisions "contains the seeds for oligopoly," and that leading mitigations (regulatory markets, open ecosystems, transparency) are "valuable but insufficient" [[doi:10.1007/s00146-025-02534-0]]. This sits in direct tension with the frontier-regulation and trustworthy-AI agendas [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2307.03718]], [[doi:10.1016/j.inffus.2023.101896]]. The frontier-regulation authors themselves flag regulatory capture and innovation-chilling as risks of their own proposal [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2307.03718]].

6. **Short-term/specific vs. long-term/general framing.** The corpus explicitly spans "short-term specific system engineering to long-term artificial general intelligence," with acknowledged disagreement about priorities [[doi:10.1109/dsn-w50199.2020.00023]] and proposals to treat generality, capability, and control as quantitative degrees rather than binary categories [[openalex:W3013683725]]. The choice of AI paradigm (e.g., RL vs. others) itself shapes which safety issues are even visible [[doi:10.3233/faia200386]].

## Open Problems

- **Defining the regulatory object.** No definitional approach for "frontier AI," including compute thresholds, is "fully satisfying," leaving the trigger for stringent regulation unresolved [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2307.03718]].
- **Quantitative risk assessment.** The field "lacks detailed understanding of how harms could materialize," and current methods cannot rigorously show that control thresholds keep risk below tolerance; high-assurance guarantees for LLMs "have not yet been demonstrated" [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2502.06656]].
- **Evaluation incompleteness.** Model evaluation is "necessary but not sufficient": it cannot detect all risks (capability overhang, deceptive alignment, scale-emergent capabilities), is value-laden, and to be impactful must be regular, consequential, standardized, and independent [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2305.15324]], [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2310.11986]]. External evaluators face limited access, information, and time, with no common access taxonomy [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2601.11916]].
- **Decorrelating safety metrics from capabilities.** Future safety benchmarks should report capabilities correlation and be designed to be decorrelated, since "intuitive verbal arguments are a poor predictor of empirical correlations" [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2407.21792]].
- **Interpretability.** Whether truly interpretable deep-learning systems can be built and deployed "remains unclear," and the field lacks a common vocabulary to measure progress [[doi:10.51593/20190042]].
- **Instrumenting hidden, systemic, and multimodal failures.** Distributed, normalized, and non-text failure modes remain largely un-instrumented [[doi:10.1007/s43681-026-01132-0]], [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2310.11986]].
- **Governance coverage and enforcement gaps.** Standardized assessment procedures, incident-reporting taxonomies, and enforceable certification are missing in most jurisdictions, with disproportionate burden on SMEs [[doi:10.1108/tg-03-2025-0065]]; governance datasets remain US- and English-skewed [[doi:10.1609/aies.v7i1.31615]]; and international standardization is slow and may not reflect all countries' contexts [[doi:10.1057/s41599-024-03017-1]].
- **Agentic alignment.** Reward modeling, scalable oversight, corrigibility, deceptive alignment, and value drift for autonomous agents are unresolved technical problems [[doi:10.1093/oxfordhb/9780198940272.013.0006]] — and notably, the deceptive-misalignment threat model driving much of this concern is itself acknowledged as currently hypothetical [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2312.08039]].
- **Empirical test for capture.** Whether AI-safety regulation becomes captured is a "contingent empirical hypothesis" with no formal detection test and no clear existing example, leaving the strongest critique of the regulatory agenda unfalsified for now [[doi:10.1007/s00146-025-02534-0]].

## Synthesis

The intersection of AI safety and AI policy is a young, fast-growing, methodologically conceptual field that has converged on a shared problem structure — robustness/assurance/specification at the technical layer [[doi:10.51593/20190040]]; unexpected-capabilities / control / proliferation at the frontier-governance layer [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2307.03718]]; and risk-tolerance-driven, evaluation-centered management bridging the two [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2502.06656]], [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2305.15324]]. What is genuinely *established* is mostly diagnostic: that ML lacks classical safety guarantees, that interpretability and evaluation are necessary but immature, that safety benchmarks can be confounded by capabilities, and that governance is fragmented and needs interoperable, multi-layer coordination. What remains *open* is almost everything operational: defining the regulated object, quantifying risk, decorrelating safety metrics, instrumenting hidden failures, aligning agentic systems, and preventing regulatory capture. The field's defining tension — captured most sharply in the safetywashing and regulatory-capture critiques — is that both its technical measurements and its policy instruments are vulnerable to producing the *appearance* of safety without the substance, a meta-problem the corpus is only beginning to instrument [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2407.21792]], [[doi:10.1007/s00146-025-02534-0]], [[doi:10.1007/s43681-026-01132-0]].
