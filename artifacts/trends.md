# Trends & Controversy

- **Rising corpus volume and a decisive pivot to frontier / general-purpose AI (2023 onward)** — The year_hist shows publication output roughly tripling from the 2018-2022 plateau (33-67/yr) to a 2023-2026 surge (130, 220, 243, 174), inflecting sharply with ChatGPT-era general-purpose models. The newer cohort reframes safety around 'frontier'/foundation models, dangerous-capability thresholds, and pre-deployment risk management rather than narrow ML failure modes, and several pieces explicitly synthesize the state of the field (Singapore Consensus, the First International AI Safety Report). This is the dominant trajectory in the corpus. [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2307.03718]] [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2502.06656]] [[doi:10.70777/si.v2i5.15503]] [[doi:10.70777/si.v2i2.14757]] [[doi:10.3389/fpos.2025.1561776]] [[doi:10.1093/oxfordhb/9780198940272.013.0006]]
- **Model evaluations, dangerous-capability testing, and the science of 'evals'** — A fast-growing thread treats pre-deployment model evaluation as the central governance lever: identifying dangerous capabilities and harmful propensities, embedding evals into governance, and expanding rigorous external/structured access to frontier models. The 2024-2026 items push toward an evaluation 'science' (taxonomies of access levels, sociotechnical multi-layer evaluation, system-level rather than model-only evaluation). This trend is rising and increasingly operational/policy-linked (e.g., the EU GPAI Code of Practice). [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2305.15324]] [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2601.11916]] [[doi:10.1145/3664646.3664766]] [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2310.11986]] [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2407.21792]]
- **Convergence of AI safety with AI security (and risk management)** — Multiple recent papers argue that safety (preventing harm to the environment, accidental failures) and security (protecting the system from adversaries) must be unified under AI risk management, noting security has historically been marginalized in LLM safety work. Related governance-safety-security 'controls' framings and risk-management bridging papers reflect a maturing trend toward integrated, lifecycle, high-risk-industry-style risk management. [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2405.19524]] [[doi:10.34190/icair.4.1.3142]] [[doi:10.4018/979-8-3693-3860-5.ch011]] [[doi:10.7753/ijcatr1502.1003]] [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2502.06656]]
- **Proliferation of AI governance frameworks, standards, certification, and risk-based regulation** — A large and steadily growing governance cluster proposes layered frameworks linking regulation to standards, certification, audits, and conformity assessment, frequently anchored to the EU AI Act's risk-based approach and to interoperability across the EU/US/UK/China. Systematic literature reviews of AI governance recur, signaling field consolidation. The trajectory tracks the broad post-2023 surge as regulation moves from principles to implementation. [[doi:10.1108/tg-03-2025-0065]] [[doi:10.1109/i-coste68047.2025.11467397]] [[doi:10.1609/aies.v7i1.31615]] [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2401.10896]] [[doi:10.1007/s43681-024-00653-w]] [[doi:10.1787/ba602d18-en]]
- **Sociotechnical and systems-safety reframing of AI safety** — An increasingly prominent strand argues safety is an emergent property of the whole deployment stack and sociotechnical system, not a scalar property of a model. It revisits 'concrete problems' through real-world incident analysis, foregrounds hidden/quiet failures and overreliance, and pushes for system-level (not model-only) evaluation and governance. These contributions cluster in 2023-2026. [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2401.10899]] [[doi:10.1007/s43681-026-01132-0]] [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2310.11986]] [[doi:10.1145/3664646.3664766]]
- **AI alignment formalization: taxonomies, scalable oversight, and interpretability** — Alignment research is consolidating into structured reviews and taxonomies (alignment cycles, disentangling alignment from safety/ethics) while emphasizing scalable oversight, corrigibility, robustness, interpretability, and control. Interpretability/control/robustness are repeatedly named as core near-term investment areas tied to policy initiatives. This is a coherent, growing technical-safety trend in the corpus. [[doi:10.1142/s021800142539001x]] [[doi:10.1007/978-3-032-01377-4_8]] [[doi:10.3390/a19020136]] [[doi:10.51593/20190042]] [[doi:10.1093/oxfordhb/9780198940272.013.0006]]
- **Internationalization and institutional design of AI safety governance** — Recent work pursues cross-jurisdictional and international mechanisms: IAEA/nuclear-style analogies, proposals for independent certification authorities and global oversight bodies, comparative EU/US/UK/China studies, and field-priority consensus documents. The push toward harmonization, interoperability, and treaty-style architecture is a clear 2024-2026 trajectory. [[doi:10.1057/s41599-024-03017-1]] [[doi:10.2139/ssrn.6240398]] [[doi:10.1109/i-coste68047.2025.11467397]] [[doi:10.70777/si.v2i5.15503]] [[doi:10.1787/ba602d18-en]]
- **Compute-, agentic-, and decentralization-driven governance challenges** — Emerging at the policy-technical frontier: compute as a governance lever (visibility, allocation, enforcement via a concentrated chip supply chain), the distinct safety challenges of autonomous agentic AI, and the erosion of the data-center assumption by open-source/local models running outside regulatory view. These newer (2024-2026) items extend governance beyond deployment-time, model-centric assumptions. [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2402.08797]] [[doi:10.1093/oxfordhb/9780198940272.013.0006]] [[doi:10.3390/ai6070159]]

## Contested / open debates

- **Do safety benchmarks actually measure safety? ('safetywashing')**: A central methodological dispute: many widely used safety benchmarks correlate strongly with general capabilities and training compute, so capability gains can be misrepresented as safety progress ('safetywashing'). This contests the validity of benchmark-driven safety claims and intersects with broader concerns that evaluations are nascent, have weak construct validity, and cannot rule out harmful capabilities, undermining confidence in current measurement practice. [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2407.21792]] [[doi:10.70777/si.v2i5.15503]] [[doi:10.1145/3664646.3664766]] [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2310.11986]]
- **Regulatory capture, industry self-regulation, and 'ethics washing'**: Sharp disagreement over whether AI-safety regulation will be captured by powerful incumbents. One side warns the EU AI Act-style fixed compliance costs entrench oligopoly and that industry self-regulation/ethics serve to forestall binding rules; proposals for independent certification authorities are pitched against demonstrated self-regulation failure. Others defend industry-first or voluntary first steps, making the legitimacy and capture-resistance of regulatory design genuinely contested. [[doi:10.1007/s00146-025-02534-0]] [[doi:10.2139/ssrn.6240398]] [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2307.03718]] [[doi:10.1016/j.inffus.2023.101896]]
- **Compute thresholds as a governance strategy**: Contested whether FLOP/compute thresholds (e.g., ~1e26) are a sound trigger for frontier regulation. Proponents present a compute threshold as a simple, objective ex ante way to identify frontier models and treat compute as a governable, detectable input. Critics counter that compute correlates poorly and unstably with risk, that thresholds are scientifically unjustified and gameable (Goodhart), and that smaller models and inference-time compute undercut them. [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2307.03718]] [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2402.08797]]
- **How much risk management is 'enough'? The EU AI Act's acceptable-risk standard**: Debate over the AI Act's 'reduce risks as far as possible' (AFAP) standard versus reasonableness/cost-benefit balancing. One position argues AFAP plus a vague 'state of the art' is unworkable, disproportionate, and improperly cedes value-laden, fundamental-rights judgments to technical standards bodies; it favors reasonableness or an ALARP-style compromise. This exposes a deeper, unresolved dispute about whether risk-acceptability can be quantified at all. [[doi:10.1017/err.2023.57]] [[doi:10.1109/i-coste68047.2025.11467397]] [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2502.06656]]
- **Existential / loss-of-control risk versus present, sociotechnical harms**: A persistent tension over where safety attention belongs. Some frame catastrophic, loss-of-control, and dangerous-capability scenarios as the priority; others argue this focus is incomplete or distracting, emphasizing real-world deployment incidents, hidden/quiet failures, and sociotechnical harms, and noting loss-of-control likelihood is itself debated among experts. Whether AGI/existential framing should even be in scope is explicitly disputed. [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2307.03718]] [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2401.10899]] [[doi:10.1007/s43681-026-01132-0]] [[doi:10.70777/si.v2i2.14757]] [[doi:10.1201/9781003530336]]
- **Conceptual boundaries: what is 'AI safety' (vs security, ethics, alignment, robustness)?**: The field disagrees on its own definitions. Papers note safety is poorly defined and inconsistently measured; some argue safety must be distinguished from (yet integrated with) security, that safety does not entail ethicality and a model can be robust yet unsafe, and that alignment, machine ethics, and safety have vague, overlapping boundaries. Even the meaning of 'safe' is treated as inherently value-laden and contested. [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2407.21792]] [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2405.19524]] [[doi:10.1007/978-3-032-01377-4_8]] [[doi:10.1109/dsn-w50199.2020.00023]] [[doi:10.7753/ijcatr1502.1003]]
- **International architecture: an 'IAEA for AI' / global authority vs polycentric or market-based governance**: Competing visions for global governance. Some advocate IAEA-style standardized international safety regulation or an independent global certification/oversight authority; others argue nuclear and AI are dissimilar policy problems, that a centralized 'IAEA for AI' is unlikely to work, and that strengthening a weak polycentric regime complex (or regulatory-markets approaches) is more realistic. The right institutional form is openly unsettled. [[doi:10.1057/s41599-024-03017-1]] [[doi:10.2139/ssrn.6240398]] [[doi:10.1080/09700161.2023.2288994]] [[doi:10.3389/fpos.2025.1561776]]
- **Decentralized / open-source models and the limits of model-centric, deployment-time governance**: Contested whether prevailing governance assumptions hold as open-source and local models run outside data centers and regulatory visibility, stripped of safety constraints. This interacts with disputes over external/structured model access for evaluations (deeper access improves rigor but raises IP/security leakage and may not be granted) and over how to regulate downstream developers without stifling innovation. [[doi:10.3390/ai6070159]] [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2601.11916]] [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2402.08797]]
