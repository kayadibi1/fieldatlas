# Trends & Controversy

- **Post-ChatGPT surge and pivot to frontier/general-purpose AI safety** — After a 2020-2022 dip (year_hist: 105/86/73), the corpus shows a sharp rebound from 2023 (112) peaking in 2025 (148), with 2026 already at 87 by mid-year. This inflection coincides with the generative-AI era and a thematic shift from narrow ML-safety concepts toward 'frontier AI' and general-purpose models capable of dangerous capabilities. The framing crystallized around proactive risk management before deployment and dedicated frontier-model regulation. [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2307.03718]] [[doi:10.3389/fpos.2025.1561776]] [[doi:10.70777/si.v2i2.14757]] [[doi:10.1162/99608f92.88b4cc98]]
- **Dangerous-capability evaluations and model evals as a governance instrument** — A fast-growing trend treats pre-deployment model evaluation as the central technical mechanism for governing extreme risks (offensive cyber, manipulation, etc.). Recent work (2026) extends this to the operational problem of how much external/third-party access evaluators need, reflecting maturation from 'we should evaluate' (2023) to 'how do we operationalize rigorous external evals' (2026), explicitly tied to instruments like the EU GPAI Code of Practice. [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2305.15324]] [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2601.11916]] [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2307.03718]]
- **Convergence of safety, security, and risk-management framings** — Historically separate communities (AI safety = accidental/non-adversarial harm; AI security = adversarial attacks) are increasingly merged under a unified 'AI risk management' umbrella, driven by LLM jailbreaks exposing the safety-security interplay. A parallel governance/security/safety-controls vocabulary is emerging (2026), signaling consolidation of previously siloed disciplines. [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2405.19524]] [[doi:10.7753/ijcatr1502.1003]] [[doi:10.1109/access.2024.3440647]]
- **Compute and supply-chain levers as concrete governance mechanisms** — Beyond abstract principles, the corpus shows growing interest in tangible levers—especially compute ('compute governance') and FLOP thresholds—as ex-ante, objective ways to identify and govern frontier models. This represents a move from soft ethics frameworks toward enforceable, measurable triggers for regulatory visibility. [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2402.08797]] [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2307.03718]]
- **Agentic AI safety and the alignment research consolidation** — As systems gain autonomy, safety attention is shifting to agentic AI (autonomous goal-pursuit, tool use, persistent memory) with challenges like deceptive alignment, scalable oversight, corrigibility, and value drift. The alignment subfield is simultaneously consolidating into comprehensive surveys organized around unifying principles (e.g., Robustness/Interpretability/Controllability/Ethicality), indicating field maturation. [[doi:10.1093/oxfordhb/9780198940272.013.0006]] [[doi:10.1145/3770749]] [[doi:10.1007/s43681-026-01132-0]]
- **Interpretability, control, and robustness elevated as policy priorities** — Interpretability has moved from a niche ML-research concept (2021 'Key Concepts' series) to an explicit policy axis, with 2026 work synthesizing interpretability/control/robustness directly in response to government initiatives (e.g., the White House AI Action Plan). Opacity of frontier models is now framed as a compliance and public-trust problem, not just a research curiosity. [[doi:10.3390/a19020136]] [[doi:10.51593/20190042]] [[doi:10.51593/20190040]]
- **Comparative, cross-jurisdictional and international governance frameworks** — A strong and growing strand performs cross-regional comparison (EU/US/UK/China), national case studies (Canada, Singapore), and borrows mature-sector analogies (notably the IAEA/nuclear model) to argue for standardized, internationally coordinated AI-safety regulation amid a fragmented, inconsistent global landscape. [[doi:10.1109/i-coste68047.2025.11467397]] [[doi:10.1057/s41599-024-03017-1]] [[doi:10.1017/cfl.2024.12]] [[doi:10.1016/j.giq.2024.101929]]
- **Shift from spectacular/catastrophic failures to quiet socio-technical and deployment-stack failures** — Recent (2026) perspective work reframes safety as an emergent property of the whole deployment stack rather than a scalar property of a model, emphasizing hidden, distributed, normalized failures (overreliance, RAG errors, prompt injection, agentic tool use) and expanded socio-technical framings of 'concrete problems'—a maturation beyond both pure-ML and pure-catastrophe framings. [[doi:10.1007/s43681-026-01132-0]] [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2401.10899]]

## Contested / open debates

- **Regulatory capture: does AI-safety regulation entrench incumbents?**: A central contested topic is whether safety regulation, though broadly supported, will be captured by powerful AI firms to entrench oligopoly. Critics argue frameworks like the EU AI Act ignore firm size so fixed compliance costs crush small entrants, producing distributive injustice and 'ethics washing.' This directly tensions with the pro-regulation camp that proposes licensing, registration, and supervisory enforcement for frontier models—measures capture-critics warn could backfire. [[doi:10.1007/s00146-025-02534-0]] [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2307.03718]]
- **Open-source / decentralized AI vs. controllable, centralized governance**: There is a live tension between governance models assuming large, centralized data-center providers and the reality of open-source models running locally, 'invisible to regulators and stripped of safety constraints.' Frontier-regulation proposals treat proliferation as a core risk to be contained, while decentralization scholarship argues current safeguards are being structurally bypassed—raising unresolved questions about enforceability and whether containment is even feasible. [[doi:10.3390/ai6070159]] [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2307.03718]] [[doi:10.1108/978-1-83549-001-320241007]]
- **Existential/long-term risk framing vs. present-day socio-technical harms**: The corpus reflects a long-running dispute over emphasis: catastrophic/superintelligence and AI-alignment-boundary 'existential threat' framings versus arguments that discourse over-focuses on spectacular hypotheticals and neglects quieter, already-deployed harms. Some position long-term loss-of-control as paramount; others explicitly argue this focus is incomplete and mis-prioritized. [[doi:10.22541/au.171697103.39692698/v1]] [[doi:10.1007/s43681-026-01132-0]] [[openalex:W3013683725]] [[doi:10.1201/9781003530336]]
- **Whether 'frontier AI' / compute-threshold definitions are workable for regulation**: Defining the regulatory target is contested. The frontier-regulation proposal itself concedes no definitional approach—including compute (FLOP) thresholds—is fully satisfying or precise enough for regulation, while parallel work disputes whether capability/generality/control can be meaningfully quantified at all. This unresolved definitional gap undercuts the enforceable, threshold-based mechanisms the field is converging on. [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2307.03718]] [[openalex:W3013683725]] [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2402.08797]]
- **Self-regulation / voluntary principles vs. binding government mandates**: A persistent fault line concerns whether industry self-regulation, voluntary ethics principles, and pattern catalogues suffice, or whether binding mandates, licensure, and enforcement are required. Proponents of frontier regulation call self-regulation a useful but insufficient first step; meanwhile a large 'responsible AI / principles' literature is critiqued for leaving practitioners 'with nothing much beyond truisms,' fueling debate over the principles-to-practice gap. [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2307.03718]] [[doi:10.1145/3626234]] [[doi:10.1016/j.inffus.2023.101896]]
- **Safety vs. security: distinct objectives or one risk-management field?**: Whether AI safety and AI security are separate disciplines or should be unified is itself contested. One position stresses they have differing threat models (non-adversarial vs. adversarial) and can technically clash—e.g., adversarial training improves worst-case robustness while reducing benign accuracy—so they must be accounted for separately; others fold both into a single AI-risk-management or governance-controls frame, risking omission of one perspective. [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2405.19524]] [[doi:10.7753/ijcatr1502.1003]]
- **Transparency/post-hoc accountability vs. pre-execution control architectures**: An emerging dispute (2026) challenges the dominant governance paradigm built on transparency, explainability, and post-hoc accountability, arguing these document but do not control whether AI outputs are actually executed in the real world, and instead advancing pre-execution control analogous to high-risk physical systems. This contests the explainability-centric policy consensus dominant in EU/US/UK frameworks. [[doi:10.2139/ssrn.6506258]] [[doi:10.1145/3593013.3594074]] [[doi:10.3390/a19020136]]
- **Borrowing mature-sector regulatory models (nuclear/IAEA) vs. AI-specific frameworks**: Scholars dispute whether analogies to nuclear (IAEA), aviation, or medical-device regulation are transferable to AI. Proponents draw concrete lessons (standardized criteria, neutral monitoring bodies, information-sharing), but the same and related works caution that AI's different characteristics, speed, and diversity limit direct application and may require an independent, AI-specific regime—leaving the right institutional template unresolved. [[doi:10.1057/s41599-024-03017-1]] [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2405.19524]]
