# Trends & Controversy

- **Explosive post-2022 growth of the AI safety/policy field, centered on generative and frontier models** — The year_hist shows a sharp inflection: output roughly doubled from ~60 papers/year in 2019-2022 to 130 in 2023, then surged to 220 (2024) and 243 (2025), with 2026 already at 174 (partial). This generative-AI inflection reorients the field away from pre-2022 narrow-ML safety concerns (robustness, adversarial examples) toward frontier/foundation-model risks. Foundational primers and bibliometric surveys from the field's earlier phase (e.g., the CSET Key Concepts series, the quantitative-lens survey) now sit alongside a wave of frontier-specific work, confirming a field shifting from establishing concepts to managing deployed large models. [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2307.03718]] [[doi:10.51593/20190040]] [[doi:10.51593/20210026]] [[doi:10.23919/mipro48935.2020.9245153]] [[doi:10.1109/access.2024.3440647]]
- **Frontier AI regulation and risk-management frameworks** — A dominant and fast-growing trend is the call to regulate 'frontier'/foundation models with dangerous capabilities, proposing building blocks such as standard-setting, registration/reporting, compute (FLOP) thresholds, and licensing. Recent work (2025-2026) pushes from high-level proposals toward operational rigor borrowed from mature high-risk industries (aviation, nuclear): quantitative risk tolerance, Key Risk/Control Indicators, probabilistic risk assessment, and structured external access for dangerous-capability evaluations. The 'risk, management, intelligence' cluster is one of the largest in the corpus, signaling that bringing established risk-management discipline to AI is a central organizing theme. [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2307.03718]] [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2502.06656]] [[doi:10.3389/fpos.2025.1561776]] [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2601.11916]] [[doi:10.4230/oasics.saia.2024.4]]
- **Model evaluation, dangerous-capability testing, and external/third-party access** — Evaluations have become the field's primary safety-assurance instrument. Work moves from dangerous-capability and alignment evaluations for extreme risks toward sociotechnical, multi-layer evaluation (capability + human interaction + systemic impact) and harmonized system-level (not just model-level) evaluation terminology and lifecycle mapping. The newest 2026 work formalizes taxonomies of evaluator access for external/independent dangerous-capability assessments, reflecting a shift toward institutionalized, independent auditing as a governance mechanism rather than developer self-assessment. [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2305.15324]] [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2310.11986]] [[doi:10.1145/3664646.3664766]] [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2601.11916]]
- **Convergence/disentangling of safety, security, alignment, and ethics** — A strong conceptual trend works to disentangle and re-integrate adjacent terms whose boundaries are blurry: safety vs. security (historically separate disciplines now merged under 'risk management'), and alignment vs. safety vs. machine ethics. Papers argue risk management must explicitly cover both safety (harm to the environment) and security (adversarial harm to the system), and propose structured taxonomies separating alignment's aim, scope, and constituency from ethics and safety. This terminological harmonization effort recurs across the corpus as the field matures and communities (ML, software engineering, governance) collide. [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2405.19524]] [[doi:10.34190/icair.4.1.3142]] [[doi:10.1007/978-3-032-01377-4_8]] [[doi:10.1145/3664646.3664766]]
- **Shift to sociotechnical, system-level, and deployment-stack safety** — Newer work (2023-2026) reframes safety as an emergent property of the whole deployment stack rather than a scalar property of a model. It revisits 'concrete problems' through an expanded socio-technical lens, foregrounds quieter distributed failures (overreliance, RAG errors, prompt injection, agentic tool use, persistent memory, model collapse) that current discourse fails to instrument, and stresses that context co-determines whether a capability causes harm. This represents a maturation from model-centric technical safety toward operational, in-deployment reliability. [[doi:10.1007/s43681-026-01132-0]] [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2401.10899]] [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2310.11986]] [[doi:10.1145/3664646.3664766]]
- **Agentic AI safety and autonomous systems** — An emerging late-stage trend (2025-2026) focuses on agentic AI: systems that autonomously pursue goals with limited supervision. This raises technical challenges (unintended optimization, deceptive alignment, value drift, scalable oversight, corrigibility, reward modeling) and governance challenges distinct from those for static models. Related governance proposals push 'pre-execution control' architectures analogous to high-risk physical systems, marking a move beyond transparency/post-hoc accountability toward controlling whether AI outputs are executed in the real world. [[doi:10.1093/oxfordhb/9780198940272.013.0006]] [[doi:10.2139/ssrn.6506258]] [[doi:10.1007/s43681-026-01132-0]]
- **International coordination, standards, certification, and cross-jurisdictional governance** — A substantial governance trend seeks interoperable, internationally coordinated AI governance: drawing lessons from IAEA nuclear safety, proposing layered frameworks linking regulation-standards-assessment-certification, comparing EU/US/UK/China risk-management regimes, and building analytic infrastructure (e.g., regulatory archives/datasets) to track the rapidly proliferating policy landscape. The two large 'governance' and 'policy' clusters dominate the corpus, indicating governance is at least as central as technical safety, with the EU AI Act and risk-based regulation as recurring reference points. [[doi:10.1057/s41599-024-03017-1]] [[doi:10.1108/tg-03-2025-0065]] [[doi:10.1109/i-coste68047.2025.11467397]] [[doi:10.1609/aies.v7i1.31615]] [[doi:10.1787/ba602d18-en]]

## Contested / open debates

- **Does AI safety regulation serve the public or entrench incumbents (regulatory capture)?**: A pointed contention is that ostensibly safety-oriented frontier-AI regulation is highly vulnerable to regulatory capture: rules without provisions for firm size impose fixed compliance costs that fall disproportionately on small firms, seeding oligopoly and distributive/democratic harms. This directly tensions with proposals advocating licensing, registration, and stringent standards for frontier models. Frontier-regulation proponents themselves flag regulatory capture and centralization of power as risks, making this an explicitly contested fault line about whether stringent safety regulation helps or harms the public interest. [[doi:10.1007/s00146-025-02534-0]] [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2307.03718]] [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2312.08039]]
- **Do safety benchmarks actually measure safety ('safetywashing')?**: A meta-analysis argues many AI safety benchmarks are highly correlated with general capabilities and training compute, so capability gains can be misrepresented as safety progress ('safetywashing'). This contests the field's heavy reliance on benchmarks and evaluations as the primary assurance mechanism: if alignment-preference, truthfulness, and scalable-oversight benchmarks track capability, then evaluation-centric governance proposals may be measuring the wrong thing. It also disputes the intuitive assumption that increasing capability makes models safer, showing capability can simultaneously raise weaponization/misuse risk. [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2407.21792]] [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2305.15324]] [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2310.11986]]
- **Can alignment efforts be counterproductive and increase risk?**: It is contested whether promoting AI alignment is straightforwardly beneficial. One analysis identifies mechanisms (relative-progress, false sense of security, 'dangerous valley', and 'bad principal' problems) by which well-intentioned alignment work can increase large-scale risk, e.g., by advancing capabilities faster than safeguards or by helping a malicious principal. The paper notes its own reviewers contested which alignment efforts are highest-value, and the deceptive/scheming threat model it relies on is acknowledged as currently hypothetical, underscoring live disagreement about alignment's net effect. [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2312.08039]] [[doi:10.1142/s021800142539001x]] [[doi:10.22541/au.171697103.39692698/v1]]
- **Existential/long-term superintelligence risk vs. near-term deployed harms**: A persistent framing dispute pits long-term, catastrophic/existential and superintelligence-oriented risk against near-term, concrete harms of deployed systems (bias, misinformation, privacy, overreliance, quiet distributed failures). Some work centers extreme risks and dangerous-capability/AGI scenarios; other work argues the discourse over-focuses on dramatic, hypothetical, or visible failures while neglecting the quieter, system-level failures that actually dominate deployment. The corpus's separate 'existential' cluster alongside large near-term governance/ethics clusters reflects this unresolved split over where attention and regulation should go. [[doi:10.1007/s43681-026-01132-0]] [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2305.15324]] [[openalex:W3013683725]] [[doi:10.1109/dsn-w50199.2020.00023]]
- **Is AI safety even a well-defined, coherent field?**: There is explicit disagreement over the field's basic terminology, ontology, and priorities. Multiple works note major disagreement in definitions and priorities across mainstream AI, critical-systems engineering, and AGI communities; that AI safety remains 'poorly defined and inconsistently measured'; and that the conceptual boundaries among safety, alignment, and machine ethics remain vague. The recurring push for harmonized terminology and structured taxonomies is itself evidence that what counts as 'AI safety' is contested rather than settled. [[doi:10.1109/dsn-w50199.2020.00023]] [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2407.21792]] [[doi:10.1007/978-3-032-01377-4_8]] [[doi:10.1145/3664646.3664766]]
- **Safety vs. security: distinct objectives that can technically clash**: Whether safety and security should be unified or kept distinct is contested, and the two can technically conflict: measures that improve worst-case adversarial robustness (security), such as adversarial training and randomized smoothing, can sacrifice benign accuracy and make models less safe to use. Security is also argued to be marginalized relative to safety in industry technical reports. This creates a genuine trade-off disputed across papers that otherwise agree both objectives matter, complicating unified risk-management frameworks. [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2405.19524]] [[doi:10.34190/icair.4.1.3142]] [[doi:10.3389/fpos.2025.1561776]]
- **How prescriptive should regulation be: stringent licensing/compute thresholds vs. flexible, decentralized, innovation-preserving approaches?**: The corpus disagrees on regulatory form. Some advocate stringent, ex ante mechanisms (licensing of development/deployment, compute thresholds, registration, IAEA-style international standardization). Others argue most countries do not follow EU ideals and call for pragmatic, decentralized, security-first blueprints, or warn that the EU AI Act's lack of size provisions burdens SMEs and entrenches incumbents. Even where stringent regulation is proposed, authors concede definitions (including compute thresholds) are imperfect and risk regulatory flight, leaving the appropriate stringency and locus of regulation genuinely contested. [[doi:10.48550/arxiv.2307.03718]] [[doi:10.3389/fpos.2025.1561776]] [[doi:10.1057/s41599-024-03017-1]] [[doi:10.1007/s00146-025-02534-0]] [[doi:10.1016/j.inffus.2023.101896]]
